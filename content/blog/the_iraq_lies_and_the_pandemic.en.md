In February 2003 the American Secretary of State sat before the United Nations and presented evidence that Iraq held weapons of mass destruction. He held up a vial. He showed satellite photographs with annotations. He spoke with the authority of a decorated general who had been, until then, the most trusted man in the administration.

There were no such weapons. The man himself later called the presentation a blot on his record. The people around him, the President, the Vice President, the Secretary of Defence, had wanted the war and had assembled the case to fit it.

The usual accounting of that lie counts the dead in Iraq: the soldiers who did not come back, and the far larger number of Iraqis killed in the war and the collapse that followed. I want to argue that the accounting is incomplete. **Some of the people who died of a respiratory virus seventeen years later belong on the same ledger.**

## The Public Does Not Compartmentalise

Here is the mechanism, and it is psychological rather than political.

Governments think of themselves as departments. The people who ran the intelligence case for Iraq had nothing to do with the people who would later run vaccine approval, and they would tell you, sincerely, that trust in one has nothing to do with trust in the other. Different buildings, different careers, different decades.

The public does not experience it that way. To a citizen, the government is one thing. It is the voice on the television that tells you what is true and what you must do. When that voice is caught fabricating the case for a war, the citizen does not conclude that the Office of the Secretary of State is unreliable on questions of Middle Eastern armaments. **The citizen concludes that the government lies when it wants something.**

That conclusion is then available, fully formed, the next time the same voice says something urgent. It does not matter that the subject is unrelated. It does not matter that the people are different. The trust was held in one account, and the account was drained.

## What Happened When It Was Needed

Roll forward to 2020. A government asks its population to do several difficult things on the strength of its word: stay home, close your business, take a vaccine developed in under a year. There is no way to make this work without trust. The evidence cannot be checked by the people asked to act on it, and the request comes from the same institution that once held up a vial.

A large minority declined. The refusal was scattered across many reasons, but underneath most of them was a single sentence, and I heard it many times: they lied before, why would I believe them now.

Some of the people who said that died. Some of the people they infected died. I cannot tell you the number, and I am suspicious of anyone who claims to. But the direction is not in doubt, and the causal chain is short: a lie in 2003, a reservoir of distrust, a refusal in 2020, a death.

So the men who assembled the case for Iraq are responsible not only for the dead of that war. **They spent, in one afternoon, a resource their successors would need to save lives in a different century, on a different continent of the government, for a different purpose.** They did not know they were spending it. That is not a defence. Nobody who empties a reservoir gets to say they did not know there would be a drought.

## Why Trust Is the Thing Being Destroyed

I want to generalise, because the Iraq case is only the cleanest example.

Trust in government is not a feeling. It is a capacity: the population's willingness to act on the government's word without independent verification. Every society needs that capacity for the things that cannot wait for verification. Evacuate the coast. Boil the water. Take the vaccine. Report for the draft. The government that has it can move a population in a day. The government that lacks it has to argue, and argument is slow, and people die in the gap.

That capacity is built slowly and spent fast. A single visible lie, on a matter of consequence, spends more of it than a decade of quiet competence rebuilds. And because the public holds it in one account, **any part of the government can spend what every other part will need.**

## The System Does Not Protect the Account

Here is why this is a structural post and not a post about four men.

Our system of government gives no one the job of protecting the trust account. The administration that spends it will not be in office when the bill comes due. The minister who lies to win a war is judged on the war, not on the pandemic response of a government fifteen years later. Every incentive he faces points at the immediate objective, and none point at the resource he is depleting for others.

I have written about how the system [selects for people who ask what wins rather than what is true](@/blog/politics_asks_what_wins.en.md), and about how [being a known liar is an advantage](@/blog/known_liar_advantage.en.md) inside it. The Iraq-to-pandemic chain is those two arguments with a body count attached. Lying won the argument in 2003. The cost landed on strangers, later, in a way no one in the room would ever be blamed for.

A system in which the people who spend public trust never pay for spending it will always run the account down to zero, and will then be surprised when nobody listens in an emergency. There is nothing mysterious about the surprise. It is what the design produces.

## What Follows

Two things.

First, the demand for honesty in government is not a moral nicety. It is public health, national defence and disaster response, all of which run on the same reservoir. A government that lies about anything is degrading its capacity to do everything.

Second, no appeal to the conscience of individual officials will fix this, because the incentive is structural. The only remedy is a structure in which the people making the claims are the people who bear the cost of being disbelieved later. That is a very different structure from the one we have, and the pandemic dead are part of the argument for it.
