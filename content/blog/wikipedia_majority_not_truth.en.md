I spent a long time arguing with the editors of the Hebrew Wikipedia about whether the Exodus should be described as non-historical. The argument taught me more about Wikipedia than about the Exodus.

## What the Argument Revealed

Wikipedia is a political organisation. Not in the sense of having a party line, but in the sense that its content is settled by a process in which **numbers and persistence matter more than evidence.**

Within that process, majority opinion carries substantial weight — through consensus norms, through what counts as a reliable source, and through simple attrition. The editor who is still there after the fifteenth exchange wins.

Now add the demographic fact. In a religious country, the majority is religious. And religious editors have far more motivation to defend claims about their own scripture than sceptics have to contest them: it matters to them personally, in a way it does not to someone who merely thinks the archaeology is clear.

So this is a domain where the religious majority actually prevails. It does not prevail in the courts or in the economy. **It prevails here, because here the currency is sustained attention, and they have more of it.**

## Why This Is a Problem

Wikipedia is supposed to be the common source of knowledge. It is the first result, the thing students cite, the thing that trains language models, and increasingly the thing that determines what a fact *is* for practical purposes.

Because it is edited by majorities, it delivers **majority opinion where the two diverge** — and it delivers it with the presentation and authority of a reference work.

On most subjects this does not matter, because on most subjects the majority is not invested and the evidence wins by default. It matters precisely on the subjects where people care, which are the subjects where getting it right matters most.

## The Proposal: A Wikipedia for Experts

A history edition editable only by professional historians. A structured process governing who qualifies, with credentials checked and editing restricted accordingly.

The practical value is enormous and immediate. Arguing a point currently requires assembling fifty papers and hundreds of links, and the person on the other side needs only persistence. With an expert edition you could point to it and say: *historians hold that the Exodus did not happen, or at minimum that there is no evidence for it.* One link, carrying the weight of the field.

## The Objections I Take Seriously

This is not a clean proposal and I want to state the problems.

**Who certifies the experts?** Any credentialing body can be captured, and a discipline that has gone wrong would encode its error with more authority rather than less.

**Fields have their own majorities.** I have argued elsewhere that academia is systematically more cautious about blaming religion than about blaming anything else. An expert Wikipedia would encode that caution too — the bias is in the field, not only in the crowd.

**It surrenders something real.** The open model produces enormous coverage of subjects no expert would bother with, and its failures are at least visible in the talk pages.

So: not a solution, and possibly not even an improvement. But the current arrangement has a specific and identifiable failure mode — **it resolves contested factual questions by counting the motivated** — and that failure mode is worth naming even before anyone has a better design.

Maybe it is a good idea. I genuinely do not know. It seems worth thinking about, and I notice that almost nobody does, because saying anything critical of Wikipedia is treated as an attack on the idea of shared knowledge rather than as a comment on one implementation of it.
