Ask a football supporter why their club won on Saturday and you will get an account of tactics, character, a manager's decision, a defender's lapse. Ask a statistician the same question and you will get a shrug, because the honest answer is that in a low-scoring game decided by one or two events, the better team loses a very large fraction of the time.

Both answers are correct. The gap between them is the subject of this post.

## The Arithmetic of a Low-Scoring Game

Football scores are small. A typical match produces two or three goals in total, and a great many produce one or none. When the outcome depends on so few events, the outcome carries very little information about which side was better.

Compare basketball, where a team scores a hundred points in a game. A hundred trials average out the luck; the better team wins most of the time, and a season table tells you something close to the truth about relative strength. In football the sample per match is two or three trials. A deflection, a refereeing call, a shot that hits the post instead of going in, and the weaker side goes home with the points. Analysts who have tried to separate skill from chance in football results have generally concluded that the chance component is larger than in almost any other major sport.

**A single football match is, to a first approximation, a weighted lottery.** The better team holds more tickets. It does not hold all of them, and on any given Saturday the draw is close to fair.

## Why That Is the Feature, Not the Bug

Here is the counterintuitive part. The randomness is not a defect that the sport tolerates. It is the reason the sport can do the job its supporters actually use it for.

Consider what a supporter gets from a club. Not entertainment in the ordinary sense; the entertainment is often miserable. What they get is a self, or a large part of one. A city, a colour, a set of songs, a history of grievance against another city, a group of people to stand with on cold afternoons, a language of hope and despair to share with strangers. The club is a machine for belonging.

Now suppose the outcomes tracked quality faithfully. The rich clubs would win every match and every title, the poor clubs would lose every match, and the table would be the same every year. Within a decade the identity would be unbearable for most of the supporters, because it would be an identity built on certain and repeated defeat with no plausible story attached. People can bear losing. They cannot bear losing predictably forever.

The lottery is what saves them. Because any match can be won, every supporter of every club can, without lying to themselves, go into a Saturday with hope. Because the season has enough noise in it, a mediocre club can have a run and a great club can have a collapse, and each produces a story that a supporter will tell for the rest of their life. The randomness supplies exactly the intermittent reinforcement that keeps a habit alive, and it supplies it to everyone, not only to the winners.

**A sport that rewarded merit reliably would be a worse identity machine, because most identities would be identities of failure.** Football distributes hope roughly evenly by refusing to distribute victory according to quality.

## The Narrative Layer

The supporter's account of tactics and character is not stupid. It is the necessary second half of the mechanism.

A lottery on its own cannot bear an identity. Nobody builds a self around a raffle ticket. What makes football different from a raffle is that the random outcome is immediately clothed in a story about will, skill, courage and betrayal, and the story is what the supporter carries away. The same result that a statistician sees as a deflection off a shin is, by the evening, a tale of a team that wanted it more. Neither reading is dishonest. One is about the event and the other is about what people do with events.

I have written about how [mass mourning for a stranger](@/blog/mass_mourning_for_strangers.en.md) uses machinery built for a small group and points it at a broadcast image. Football is the same machinery running in the other direction: it manufactures a small group out of a broadcast image, and it needs the outcomes to be uncertain enough that the group's story can go either way.

## What This Says About Us

The interesting fact is not about football. It is that human beings have found a way to take an almost random process and extract from it belonging, meaning, memory and a lifelong story, simply by attaching a narrative to each draw and a colour to each side.

That capacity is enormously useful and somewhat alarming. It is the same one that turns the random fortunes of a family, a nation or a religion into destiny. Football is the harmless laboratory version. It lets you watch, every weekend, how quickly a coin toss becomes a conviction, and how little the conviction depends on which way the coin fell.
