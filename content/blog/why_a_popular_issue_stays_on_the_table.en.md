Here is a situation every representative democracy contains at any given moment. There is an issue that has been controversial for a long time. It is important. And the public has, by now, made up its mind: a clear and stable majority wants it settled one way. The legislature also has a majority for that outcome.

Nothing happens. The issue stays on the table for another decade.

I want to explain why this is not an accident, and why the mechanism that produces it is one of the more revealing things about how representation works.

## The Fear

Imagine the government tries to do the obvious thing and pass the law. Two fears stop it.

The first is the minority in the public that opposes the move. They are, by construction, the losing side, but they are also the side that cares intensely, because an issue that has been controversial for years has produced organised, motivated opponents. They will protest. They will fill the media. They will make the government look as if it is trampling on people, and they will do so with far more energy than the satisfied majority will spend defending it.

The second fear is the organisations. Unions, corporations, churches, professional bodies, whichever have a stake. Their power in a representative system is out of all proportion to their numbers, because they are organised and permanent while the public is neither. If they oppose the move they can make it very expensive.

**A representative government does not weigh the majority against the minority. It weighs the noise the minority will make against the silence the majority will offer in return.** On that scale the minority usually wins, even at sixty-forty in the public.

## The Only Way Out, and Why It Is Closed

There is one move that would defuse both fears. The government could ask the public directly. Hold a vote on the single issue, win it, and then act. The protesters would be protesting against a demonstrated majority rather than against a legislature nobody trusts to represent anyone. The organisations would be fighting the people rather than a handful of ministers who can be pressured.

The government will not do this, and the reason is more interesting than cowardice.

If you consult the public once, you have admitted that the public can be consulted. The next controversial issue will bring the demand to do it again, and the one after that. Each consultation transfers a decision from the representatives to the people who elected them, and a legislature that keeps making that transfer is slowly turning the country into a direct democracy and itself into a clerk. **The move that would let the government act is the move that would make the government unnecessary.**

So the option is closed, not by law but by self-preservation. And with it closed, the issue stays on the table.

## What This Tells You

Notice what the example is not. It is not a case of the public being divided, or ignorant, or fickle. The premise is a long-standing, informed, stable majority. It is not a case of the legislature disagreeing with the public; the legislature agrees. Every condition the defenders of representation say is required for the system to work is met.

And the system still cannot act, because the mechanism that connects the public's settled will to a settled law does not exist. What exists is a body of representatives exposed to pressure, and pressure comes from the organised and the intense, not from the many.

I find this a cleaner demonstration of the problem than corruption or lobbying, because it requires no villains. Nobody in the story is bought. The representatives simply respond to the forces that actually act on them, and the majority is not one of those forces. It is a number in a poll, and a poll does not march or fund or threaten.

## The Comparison

Under direct democracy the situation does not arise. The public that has made up its mind votes, and the matter is settled by exactly the body whose settledness was the point. The protesting minority protests against a result, which is its right, but it cannot pretend the result does not represent anyone. The organisations have no handful of decision-makers to press, only a population too large to threaten and too numerous to buy.

That does not make every decision good. It makes decisions possible, which a representative government facing an intense minority cannot always say. A system that cannot clear a question off its desk even when the people it claims to represent have answered it is a system whose claim to represent them needs a different word.
