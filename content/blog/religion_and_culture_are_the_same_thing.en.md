In modern conversation, and especially inside university lecture halls, we tend to talk about "religion" and "culture" as if they are two neatly bordered countries on a map. You have your religious beliefs over here in a private, spiritual box, and you have your cultural life (food, holidays, art, norms, language, and values) over there in the public square.

It is a tidy framework. It makes for clean academic syllabus divisions and polite dinner party distinctions. There is just one problem with it: it isn't real. Religion and culture are not two separate forces that occasionally bump into each other. At their core, they are the exact same phenomenon.

## When Religion Was the Entirety of Culture

To understand why this separation rings hollow, we only need to look at history. For almost the entirety of human civilization, there was no word for "religion" as something distinct from the rest of life. Religion *was* the culture, or so close to the entirety of it that drawing a line between the two would have been incomprehensible to anyone living at the time.

Consider how human societies organized themselves for millennia:

- **Time and Calendars:** The rhythm of the week, the changing of agricultural seasons, and the markers of the year were sacred schedules. Rest days, harvests, and new years were not "cultural events with religious themes"; they were cosmic obligations.
- **Law and Morality:** What was legal, what was taboo, how contracts were sealed, and how justice was dispensed flowed directly from a society's understanding of divine order.
- **Art, Music, and Literature:** From Homer's epics and ancient Greek theater to Gothic cathedrals, classical Indian dance, and choral polyphony, human creativity wasn't merely "inspired" by religion. It was the medium through which religion lived and breathed.
- **Daily Habits:** How people greeted one another, what they ate, how they dressed, and how they mourned the dead or welcomed a newborn were all simultaneously cultural reflexes and religious acts.

For most of human history, asking someone to separate their religion from their culture would have been like asking a fish to separate wetness from water.

## From the Whole to the Main Engine

Today, the landscape looks different on the surface. Modernity introduced secular institutions, scientific inquiry, and globalized entertainment. Because of this expansion, religion is no longer 100% of culture. Instead, religion has become *part* of culture.

Yet here is the crucial truth that gets lost in modern discourse: even as a part of culture, religion remains its single most significant, foundational component. It is the bedrock beneath the topsoil.

When we talk about "culture" today, whether we mean Western individualism, Middle Eastern hospitality, East Asian filial piety, or Latin American family structures, we are still talking about religion. Our moral intuitions about human rights, guilt, forgiveness, charity, and progress did not materialize out of thin air; they are inherited theological frameworks wearing everyday clothes. Even when a society stops attending houses of worship, the holidays it celebrates, the idioms in its language, its attitudes toward work and wealth, and its deepest anxieties about right and wrong remain fundamentally religious in structure and origin.

So when we talk about culture, we are still talking about religion, because religion is the main component holding the cultural edifice together.

## The Academic Fiction of Artificial Lines

Why, then, do we insist on treating them as two distinct domains?

Much of the blame lies in academic circles (across sociology, anthropology, and political theory) where scholars have spent generations trying to dissect human behavior into sterile, manageable categories. To study society "scientifically," academia needed to isolate religion as a discrete variable: a set of private creeds and rituals that could be lifted out of the broader cultural soup and examined under a microscope.

Alongside this academic categorization came a broader modern political project: the deliberate attempt to separate religion from culture so that public life could be declared neutral and purely "cultural," while religion was relegated to the private sphere.

That project did not succeed. And it failed for a simple reason: you cannot surgically remove the skeleton from a living body and expect the body to keep standing. Because religion remains the main component of culture, the attempt to pry them apart didn't actually separate them. It only changed our vocabulary. Instead of actually divorcing culture from religion, we simply trained ourselves to *think* and *speak* as if they are two separate domains, even while living in a reality where they remain inextricably fused.

## Seeing the Whole Picture Again

Once you strip away the artificial lines drawn by theorists and institutions, the world makes a lot more sense. You stop wondering why "cultural" debates carry the fierce, uncompromising heat of religious wars, because they *are* religious at their root. You stop trying to figure out where a tradition's "religious" meaning ends and its "cultural" meaning begins.

Recognizing that religion and culture are fundamentally the same thing doesn't require abandoning modern life. It simply requires intellectual honesty. The lines between them were drawn on paper, not in reality, and the sooner we admit that religion is still the beating heart of culture, the better we will understand ourselves and the world around us.
