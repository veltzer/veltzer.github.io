Conscience presents itself as an inner voice concerned with right and wrong as such. It does not feel like calculation. When you return a dropped wallet or decline to take credit for someone else's work, it feels like responding to the moral facts, not like protecting an asset.

The evidence suggests otherwise. A large fraction of what feels like disinterested conscience is machinery for being *seen* to cooperate — evolved not to make you good, but to make you reliably appear good to an audience that is watching and keeping score. The tell is not in what people say about their motives, which is the least reliable evidence available. It is in the pattern of when moral behaviour switches on.

## The Eyes Effect

Start with the most direct evidence: moral behaviour is strongly sensitive to observation, including observation that is obviously fake.

The classic demonstration is Bateson's honesty box study. An office coffee station operated on trust, with a price list and a box for payments. Above it, alternating weekly, was either an image of flowers or an image of a pair of eyes. In the eyes weeks, contributions were substantially higher. Nobody believed a photograph was watching them. The cue was enough.

The general finding — that people give more, cheat less and litter less under cues of observation — has been replicated many times, though not uniformly, and effect sizes vary with context. Take the specific magnitudes with appropriate caution. But the direction is robust and the mechanism it implies is not subtle: something in us is tracking audience, and it adjusts behaviour accordingly, below the level of belief.

Note what this cannot be. A faculty that responded to the moral facts would not care whether anyone was looking, because being observed does not change whether taking the coffee without paying is wrong. A faculty tracking reputational exposure would respond exactly this way.

## Anonymity Changes the Numbers

The same pattern appears whenever anonymity is manipulated directly.

In the dictator game, one player divides money with a passive recipient. Under standard conditions many players give a substantial share. Increase the anonymity — so that neither the recipient nor the experimenter can connect the choice to the person — and giving falls sharply. Hoffman and colleagues showed this repeatedly: generosity is strongly a function of who can identify the giver.

Related work shows people will pay to *avoid* being in a situation where they would be asked to give — leaving by a different exit to avoid a charity solicitor, for instance. That behaviour makes no sense for an agent maximising good outcomes, and perfect sense for an agent managing the social cost of being seen to refuse.

## Why Selection Would Build This

This is exactly what you would expect from the evolutionary problem our ancestors faced, and it connects directly to the game theory.

Humans depend on repeated cooperation with a small number of long-term partners. In that setting, your fate is determined less by any single exchange than by whether others are willing to deal with you at all. Being excluded from cooperation in a hunter-gatherer band is close to a death sentence.

So the trait under selection is not "be good." It is "be chosen as a partner" — which means having a reputation for reliability. And the cheapest way to maintain that reputation, given that others are watching and that humans are relentless gossips, is to be genuinely disposed to cooperate when it might be observed, while remaining sensitive to when it is not.

Pure calculation would be a poor solution, because calculation is detectable. A partner who visibly computes whether to keep a promise is a bad bet, and people are good at spotting it. The robust solution is to install an emotional system that *feels* like principle — guilt that arrives unbidden, indignation at unfairness, discomfort at the thought of being a person who defects. Those feelings are hard to fake precisely because they are not faked. They are real, and they are built for an audience.

That is the uncomfortable core: the sincerity is not a cover story for the reputational function. The sincerity *is* the reputational mechanism. Feeling genuinely bad about cheating is how the machine makes you a credible partner.

## The Shape of Our Moral Concern

Look at how moral intuition is distributed, and it maps onto reputational relevance rather than onto harm.

We feel far more strongly about a betrayal by a friend than about a statistically larger harm done to strangers far away. We are outraged by visible free-riders — benefit fraud, queue-jumpers — far out of proportion to the damage they do, while responding weakly to diffuse harms with no identifiable cheat. We care intensely about intentions and only moderately about outcomes, which is exactly right if you are assessing what kind of *partner* someone is, and exactly wrong if you are assessing how much good was done.

A conscience calibrated to harm would be roughly indifferent to distance, visibility and intention. Ours is not remotely indifferent to any of them.

## Two Things This Does Not Show

The argument is easy to overstate, and the overstatements are false.

**It does not show that morality is fake, or that you should defect when unobserved.** The dispositions are real, they genuinely constrain behaviour, and a person with strong ones is genuinely more trustworthy. That your capacity for guilt has an evolutionary function no more makes guilt fictional than the evolutionary function of hunger makes hunger fictional.

**It does not show that all moral behaviour is reputational.** People do sacrifice for strangers they will never meet, donate anonymously, and take real risks for people who cannot repay them. The claim is about the bulk of ordinary moral motivation and its distinctive shape, not about every instance. There is genuine impartial concern in humans; it is just a smaller component than our self-image suggests, and it runs on machinery built for something else.

And there is a further move available that the origin story does not touch: you can *endorse* impartial concern on reflection, and act on it deliberately, precisely because you now understand where the default settings came from and why they are parochial. Knowing that the machinery is tuned for a hunter-gatherer audience is what lets you override it.

## The Familiar Tell

Notice the shape of the resistance this argument provokes, because it is the same shape as everywhere else.

The reflex is: *this is cynical, it degrades something noble, it cannot be right.* That is a complaint about how the finding feels, not about the evidence. And the discomfort is itself predicted by the theory — a creature whose moral emotions function partly to advertise its own quality would be expected to find the suggestion that they are advertising deeply threatening, and to resist it energetically. The indignation is the mechanism defending itself.

Which is why this belongs with the others. We are not the centre of the cosmos, not separate from the animals, not transparent to ourselves, not the uncaused authors of our choices. And our conscience, the last thing we might have hoped was purely ours, is substantially a device for being seen to be good by people whose willingness to cooperate with us decided whether we ate.

The honest version is more interesting than the flattering one. Human beings evolved a capacity for genuine, felt, costly commitment to fairness — not because fairness is written into the cosmos, but because creatures who could feel that way were the ones others would work with. That is a stranger and more impressive fact than a conscience handed down from above, and it has the advantage of being true.
