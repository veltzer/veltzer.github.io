The American Centers for Disease Control went into the pandemic as one of the most respected public-health bodies in the world and came out of it with its reputation in pieces. Slow tests, contradictory guidance, messaging shaped by what could be said rather than what was known. I am not interested here in which decision was wrong. I am interested in the shape of the failure, because it is the shape every large organisation takes in a crisis, and it suggests a fix.

**The fix is a hotfix, in the software sense: a patch you apply to a running system when there is no time to rebuild it.** Here it is. When an emergency is declared, a group of citizens chosen at random is placed inside the organisation for its duration, with the right to see every document, attend every meeting and ask every question about the problem at hand.

## What Goes Wrong in a Crisis

An organisation in normal times runs on its politics. Who reports to whom, which department owns which question, what can be said without ending a career, which finding embarrasses which director. In normal times this is tolerable, because the questions are slow and the politics has time to work itself out.

A crisis removes the time and keeps the politics. Information that would help is held because releasing it would expose a mistake. Decisions wait on a signature from someone protecting their position. The people who know the answer are three layers below the people who speak, and the layers filter. Nobody has to be malicious. I have [written elsewhere](@/blog/organizational_freedom_cost.en.md) that the higher a person climbs, the less free they are to say what they see, and a crisis is when that costs lives.

So the diagnosis is not incompetence. It is that the machinery built for peacetime is running in wartime, and the machinery includes its own defence mechanisms.

## What the Injection Does

The randomly chosen group has three properties the organisation's own people lack, and each one matters.

They have no career inside it. Nothing they learn can hurt them, and nothing they say can be punished by a promotion withheld, because they are leaving when the emergency ends. That is the freedom the executives have traded away.

They have no department. A question about testing does not belong to them any more than a question about supply chains does, so they can follow the problem across the organisational chart, which is exactly what the chart is designed to prevent.

They were not selected by anyone. There is no sponsor to please and no faction that placed them. Sortition is the only appointment mechanism that produces this, which is why I keep returning to it despite having [listed its disadvantages](@/blog/the_disadvantages_of_sortition.en.md) at length.

The mandate is access, not command. They do not run the response. They see it, all of it, and they can say what they see: to the organisation's head, to the legislature, and to the public. That is enough. Most of what goes wrong in a crisis goes wrong in the dark, and the group is a light that cannot be switched off from inside.

## The Advantages

The obvious one is speed. Information that would have taken a parliamentary inquiry two years to extract is on the table while it still matters.

Less obvious is what the mere presence does. An official who knows that a randomly chosen citizen may read the memo tomorrow writes a different memo. The withholding that seemed prudent becomes risky. The effect is like the honesty box with the picture of eyes above it: nothing is enforced and behaviour changes anyway.

And it is cheap. A few dozen people, a few months, no new institution. It is a patch, not a rewrite.

## The Disadvantages, Stated Honestly

The group will not understand the domain. Epidemiology is hard. They will ask naive questions and occasionally raise alarms about things that are fine.

I think this is less damaging than it sounds, for two reasons. First, they are not deciding, only seeing and asking, and a naive question answered in public costs an hour. Second, the failures I am trying to catch are rarely technical. Nobody at the CDC lacked epidemiologists. What was missing was someone who could say the obvious thing without paying for it.

The group can be captured. Brief them selectively, flatter them, keep them busy with tours. This is a real risk and the defence is the size of the group and the randomness of its selection: you cannot pre-arrange the capture of people you did not know would be chosen.

Leaks. Some information in an emergency genuinely should not be public. The answer is the one every legislature already uses: the group sees everything, and what it may publish is decided by rules set in advance, not by the organisation it is watching.

And the professionals will hate it. That is a cost, but it is also a signal. An organisation that cannot tolerate being observed in a crisis is telling you something about what it does in one.

## Why a Hotfix and Not a Reform

Because reform takes a decade and the next emergency will not wait for it. The permanent version of this idea is a different system of government, and I argue for that elsewhere. This is the version you can pass as a law in a month and switch on the day the sirens go off.

**Institutions fail in emergencies for the same reason they persist in peacetime: they protect themselves.** A hotfix does not cure that. It just puts someone in the room who has nothing to protect.
