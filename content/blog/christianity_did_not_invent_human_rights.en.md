When the foundations of modern human rights come up, a familiar assertion follows:
universal human dignity and rights are direct products of Christian thought. It is worth
asking a narrower and more answerable question. Did medieval Christian thinkers actually
make this argument themselves?

Look at the period between the 5th and 15th centuries and the proportion of Christian
thinkers who made this their claim is effectively zero.

The assertion that human rights are a product of Christianity is a modern retrospective
argument. Projecting it backward onto the figures of the Middle Ages is an anachronism.

## The Conceptual Anachronism

The modern political and legal framework of "human rights" - understood as subjective,
universal entitlements held by individuals against the state - did not exist during the
Middle Ages. Thinkers of that era could not have claimed that Christianity produced
something that had not yet been conceived.

Between the 5th and 15th centuries the prevailing framework was objective natural law
(*lex naturalis*), concerned with duties, cosmic order, and natural hierarchy rather
than individual liberty. Augustine in the 5th century and Aquinas in the 13th did
discuss human dignity, but strictly within theology and metaphysics. Their claims
concerned the nature of existence, causation, and objective morality. A hierarchy in
which each rank owes duties upward and downward is not a rights framework; it is close
to the opposite of one.

## The Evolution of *Ius*

The closest the medieval period came to individual rights was among 12th-century canon
lawyers such as Gratian. These scholars began a slow linguistic shift, occasionally
using the Latin *ius* - traditionally "what is objectively fair or just" - to refer
instead to a subjective faculty or power held by a person.

This is a genuinely important development, and historians of law are right to point at
it. But it was a granular move within property and legal theory, worked out by
specialists for technical reasons. It was never elevated into a claim about the unique
moral achievement of their faith, because that is not what they thought they were doing.

## Where the Claim Actually Comes From

The argument that Christianity invented human rights is a modern construct. It is
advanced primarily by 20th- and 21st-century historians and writers who argue that
secular Enlightenment humanism - which formally codified rights in the 17th and 18th
centuries - was built on medieval assumptions about the individual soul and its worth.

That is a real debate and there is something to be argued on both sides. Ideas do have
genealogies, and the genealogy of the modern individual does run through Christian
Europe. But two things get conflated when the claim is made casually. "Christian
societies were the environment in which rights doctrines were later formulated" is a
historical observation. "Christianity produced human rights" is a claim of authorship,
and it is one the alleged authors never made.

The scholars of the 5th to 15th centuries were occupied with ontology and systems of
duty. They were not claiming credit for a concept that had yet to be invented, and
handing them that credit retroactively tells us more about present-day apologetics than
about the Middle Ages.
