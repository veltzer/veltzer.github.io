When a head of state dies, a particular thing happens. Broadcasting is suspended, schedules are cleared, and a large number of people report genuine grief for someone they never met.

I want to write about the phenomenon rather than about any particular death, because the phenomenon is what interests me and because being rude about a specific person would obscure the point.

## The Thing That Needs Explaining

People did not know this person. They saw them occasionally on television, in ceremonial contexts, performing a role.

And the grief is not fake. That is what makes it worth examining. If it were mere social performance — everyone pretending because everyone else is — it would be a minor curiosity about conformity. But people report real feeling, and I believe them.

**So a large population experiences authentic bereavement for a stranger.** That is a strange fact about human psychology and it deserves an explanation rather than either mockery or participation.

## The Explanation I Favour

Our equipment for grief was built for small groups where everyone you saw regularly was someone you actually knew. Repeated exposure to a face was reliable evidence of a genuine relationship, because in that environment there was no other way to repeatedly see a face.

Broadcast media breaks that assumption comprehensively. You can now see someone weekly for forty years and have no relationship with them whatsoever. The machinery cannot tell the difference — it registers familiarity and produces attachment, because for the whole of our evolutionary history familiarity *was* attachment.

This is the same mechanism that produces parasocial relationships with presenters, actors, and podcasters. The public mourning of a monarch is that effect at national scale, with institutional reinforcement.

## What I Object To

Not the feeling. The feeling is involuntary and there is no sense in condemning people for it.

**What I object to is the machinery around it.** Every broadcaster clears its schedule, not primarily out of respect but because the audience numbers are enormous and the advertising is priced accordingly. A death becomes a product, and the grief is the raw material.

And there is the question underneath, which is the one that actually bothers me: the arrangement by which one family's private events become a national occasion is not natural or inevitable. It is maintained deliberately, at considerable expense, and it works — the attachment is real, and the institution depends on its being real.

**The institution is redundant and the emotion is manufactured for it.** Not fabricated — manufactured, in the way that any reliable industrial output is manufactured.

## The Part I Am Least Sure About

I should admit that I find this easier to see from outside than people find it from inside, and that this is exactly what someone would say who was simply insensitive to a shared social good.

The strongest case against me is that collective mourning does real work — it synchronises a population, marks time, and provides an occasion for grief that people are carrying anyway and have nowhere else to put. Perhaps the stranger is a socially licensed proxy for losses that have no ceremony of their own.

If that is right, the object of the grief hardly matters and my objection is beside the point. I do not think it is entirely right. But I hold the position more loosely than the tone of this post suggests.
