I have argued elsewhere on this blog that the Euthyphro dilemma settles the question of whether morality requires God. I said there was no third option and that there had not been one for two and a half thousand years.

I now think that was wrong — not about the conclusion, but about the argument. The conclusion stands: morality does not come from God. But Euthyphro does not establish it, and atheists who lean on it are relying on a weapon that a competent theist can take away from them. It is worth being clear about how, because the failure is instructive.

## The Dilemma, Restated

Plato's question, put in Socrates' mouth: is something good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good?

Take the first horn and morality becomes arbitrary. Whatever God commands is good by definition, so had He commanded torture for amusement, torture for amusement would be good — and every atrocity in scripture is defensible the moment it is attributed to Him. "God is good" degenerates into "God is whatever God is."

Take the second and goodness is independent of God. There is a standard He consults, and if He can consult it so can we. God becomes a well-informed advisor rather than a source, and the atheist and the theist are in the same position: both have to reason about what is good.

The dilemma looks airtight. Both horns are fatal, and there is no third.

## The Third Option

Here is the move I missed.

**God may be operating from a real definition of good — one that exists, that is not arbitrary, and that we simply cannot access.**

Notice what this does. It is not the arbitrary horn: there is a genuine standard, not divine whim, so the Canaanite slaughter is not good merely because it was ordered. And it is not the independent horn either, because the standard being independent of us does us no good at all if it is beyond our comprehension. We cannot appeal to it. We cannot check God's commands against it. We cannot cut out the middleman, because the middleman is the only party with access.

The dilemma assumed that "independent of God" entails "available to us." **It does not.** A standard can be real, objective, binding, and completely inaccessible to creatures with our cognitive equipment.

## Why This Is Not a Cheap Dodge

The obvious response is that this is special pleading — an unfalsifiable escape hatch invented to save the position. I do not think that is right, and this is why the objection deserves to be taken seriously.

The twentieth century made us thoroughly familiar with truths that are real but not available to us. Chaos theory shows deterministic systems whose future states are unpredictable in principle, not merely in practice. Gödel's incompleteness theorems show true statements unprovable within a given formal system. Quantum mechanics shows quantities that are not merely unmeasured but jointly unmeasurable. These are not mysteries awaiting a clever graduate student. They are structural limits on what a knower of a certain kind can know.

Once you have accepted that a proposition can be true and permanently beyond reach, you cannot turn around and insist that a moral standard must be either divine whim or fully transparent to human reason. **The excluded middle in Euthyphro is a hidden premise, and it is a premise the last century gave us excellent reason to doubt.**

## What Survives

So the theist can hold the third position coherently. He should not be pleased about it, because it costs him a great deal.

An inaccessible standard of goodness cannot ground any actual moral claim he wants to make. He cannot say the slaughter of the Canaanites was good, because he has just conceded he has no access to the criterion. He cannot say his scripture reliably tracks the standard, because he cannot check. He cannot argue that unbelievers lack a moral foundation, because on his own account nobody has one they can consult. He has purchased survival by evacuating the position of all content. It is now unfalsifiable, and therefore useless.

Which is where the honest atheist reply lives — not in the dilemma, but in what the dilemma's escape route costs.

## The Real Objection

Meanwhile the argument that actually works was never Euthyphro at all. It is the observation I made at the end of the earlier post, which does not depend on the dilemma in the least:

Believers do not read scripture and adopt whatever they find. If they did, they would still be defending slavery, stoning, and the subjugation of women. What they do instead is reach moral conclusions by ordinary human reasoning, and *then* select the passages that agree with them. The independent judgment does the work. The scripture is decoration applied afterwards.

That is an empirical claim about how religious people behave, and it is verifiable. It does not require a two-thousand-year-old dilemma, and it cannot be escaped by positing an inaccessible standard — because the behaviour is right there, in every congregation, every week.

**So: keep the conclusion. Drop the argument.** There is a version of the theist's position that survives Euthyphro. There is no version that survives an honest look at what believers actually do with their holy books.
