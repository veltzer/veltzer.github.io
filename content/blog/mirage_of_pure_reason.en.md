Philosophy has long styled itself the queen of the disciplines: the field that uncovers fundamental truths about reality by deduction and conceptual analysis alone. I have written before about [why that project fails](@/blog/why_philosophy_fails.en.md). Here I want to make a stronger claim. Philosophy is not merely unproductive. As it is practised and taught, it is anti-education: it trains people to think imprecisely and then rewards them for it.

## The Root Problem: Definitions That Do Not Hold Still

Every serious discipline begins by fixing its terms. A mathematician who says "group" or "continuous" means exactly one thing, and every reader knows which. A physicist who says "energy" or "force" has a quantity with units and a procedure for measuring it. Disagreements in these fields are disagreements about the world or about the proof, never about what the words mean, because that question was settled before the argument started.

Philosophy never takes that step. It works with the words of natural language — "cause", "nothing", "existence", "time", "form", "good" — as it finds them. These words evolved for survival, social coordination and the description of medium-sized objects. They are polysemous, context-dependent and elastic, and that elasticity is a feature in daily life. It is fatal in an argument. When a philosopher treats such a word as a rigid primitive, the slippage from one sense to another goes undetected: by the audience, and usually by the philosopher too.

Two celebrated arguments show the mechanism. I keep them short because the mechanism, not the examples, is the point.

## Two Short Examples

**Plato's Forms.** Many chairs share "chairness", many acts share "justice". Plato concluded that these universals must exist in a transcendent realm of perfect Forms. That is taking a word and promoting it to a thing. General nouns are compression algorithms the brain uses to group similar percepts; they are not entities waiting to be discovered beyond space and time. Aristotle already noticed that the theory cannot explain its own resemblance relation without a further Form, and so on without end. The whole edifice rests on never asking what "chairness" precisely refers to.

**The Kalam argument.** Whatever begins to exist has a cause; the universe began to exist; therefore the universe has a cause. Every case of "beginning to exist" we have ever observed is a rearrangement of existing matter under physical law. That is the entire evidence for the first premise. In the second premise the same phrase silently comes to mean the emergence of spacetime and physical law themselves, where no prior time existed. One phrase, two meanings, and the conclusion follows only if nobody notices the switch. I have argued elsewhere that [even granting the argument does not get you to God](@/blog/cosmological_argument_does_not_help.en.md); the point here is that an argument whose central term will not hold still should not be granted at all.

## What This Does to the People Who Learn It

Here is the part that matters. A student who spends years on this material is not learning a body of results, because there are none. What the student is learning is a habit of mind, and the habit is the wrong one.

- **They learn that a term can be left undefined and the argument still counts.** In every exact discipline the first question is "what precisely do you mean by that?", and an argument that cannot answer it is over. Philosophy teaches that the question is pedantic, and that a sufficiently elegant chain of reasoning can carry an undefined term all the way to a conclusion.
- **They learn that grammatical sentences are meaningful ones.** "What is north of the North Pole?" is a well-formed sentence. So is "What happened before time began?" Syntactic correctness confers no meaning on a question, but a philosophical education trains you to take such questions seriously and to produce elaborate answers to them.
- **They learn to defend a position by moving its terms.** When the definitions were never fixed, a challenged position can always be rescued by shifting what the words mean. This is the mechanism of dogmatism. An assumption wrapped in a flexible verbal formula is insulated from falsification, and the person trained to wrap assumptions this way is trained to be unfalsifiable.
- **They learn that sophistication is the product.** Because no result is ever checked against anything, the only thing that can be rewarded is the intricacy of the argument. Students learn to optimise for that, and the world gets people who are fluent, confident, and wrong in ways that cannot be pinned down.

That is why I call it anti-education. Education in an exact discipline makes you more precise than you were before. Philosophy, as taught, makes you less precise while convincing you that you have become more rigorous. It is training in a vice, mistaken for training in a virtue.

## The Alternative

The history of science shows what real progress looks like: hypotheses constrained by mathematical formalism and empirical testability, with every term operationalized before it is used. Philosophy could serve that enterprise — clarifying concepts, exposing hidden assumptions, dismantling verbal illusions — but only by adopting the discipline of exact definition it has spent its history avoiding. Until it does, learning it teaches you to mistake your own linguistic scaffolding for the structure of the universe, and to feel wiser for it.
