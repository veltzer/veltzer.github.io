There is a sentence you are not allowed to say in polite company: some
cultures are better than others. Some religions, too. Say it, and you'll be
called a bigot before you finish the thought. But I think it's true, and I
think most people quietly believe it. They just
[won't say it out loud](@/blog/2025-06-19-why-all-cultures-equal.md).

## Cultures are sets of ideas

A culture isn't a race or a bloodline. It's a set of ideas, habits, rules and
values that people pass on. A religion is the same: a body of teachings about
how to live. In practice the two can't be pulled apart. Religion shapes a
culture's laws, family life, holidays and morals, and culture shapes how a
religion is lived. So everything in this post applies to both, because they
are part of the same thing. Ideas can be judged. We do it all the time in
science, law and medicine. Nobody says all theories of disease are equally
valid, or that all legal systems are equally just. Why should ideas about how
to live together be the one area that's off-limits?

Judging a culture is not the same as judging a person. Millions of people born
into a culture reject its worst parts. Many of the fiercest critics of their
own traditions are insiders. Saying a set of ideas is worse than another is a
claim about ideas, not about anyone's worth as a human being.

## "Superior" is a statistical statement

When I say one religion or culture is superior to another, I'm not making a
mystical claim. I'm making a statistical one. Pick a measure: freedom of
speech, women's rights, rates of violence, democracy, literacy, scientific
output. Then look at how societies shaped by different cultures score on it.

Where exactly the line between "superior" and "inferior" falls isn't obvious.
It depends on which measures you pick and how much weight you give each one. A
culture can score well on one measure and badly on another. Reasonable people
can argue about the weights.

What isn't reasonable is pretending the scores are all the same. They aren't.
Cultures and religions don't get equal grades on every subject, and that is a
fact you can check. Refusing to say so isn't open-mindedness. It's refusing to
look at the data.

## Relativism collapses under its own weight

The opposing view, cultural relativism, says no culture can be judged by the
standards of another. It sounds tolerant. In practice it means you can't
condemn anything. Not slavery, not burning widows, not female genital
mutilation, not executing apostates or gay people. If those practices are part
of someone's culture, relativism has nothing to say against them.

Almost nobody actually lives by this. People who say all cultures are equal
still prefer to live in societies with free speech, equal rights for women,
and courts that don't take orders from clerics. Look at where people migrate.
The flow of people goes overwhelmingly in one direction, toward societies with
those values. People vote with their feet.

## What we can measure

If cultures differ, we should be able to see it in outcomes. And we can:

- **Freedom.** Some societies protect speech, conscience and the right to
  leave your religion. Others punish blasphemy and apostasy, sometimes with
  death.
- **Women's rights.** Some cultures treat women as equal citizens. Others
  restrict their movement, dress, testimony, inheritance and choice of spouse.
- **Violence and law.** Honor killings, blood feuds and tribal justice persist
  where those norms are strong, and fade where they weaken.
- **Prosperity and science.** The cultures that embraced open inquiry, rule of
  law and individual rights produced most of modern science and wealth.

Look at democracy. There are roughly 50 Muslim-majority countries, and only a
handful, such as Indonesia, Malaysia and Senegal, hold competitive elections
where power changes hands peacefully. Very few get Freedom House's top rating
of "Free." Senegal only earned it in the
[2025 report](https://www.france24.com/en/live-news/20250226-rights-decline-but-bright-spots-in-south-asia-freedom-house),
after voters defeated their president's attempt to delay elections.

These aren't accidents of geography. They track values, and values come from
culture and religion.

## Religions are not all the same

Religions differ in what their founding texts command and in how their
followers apply them today. Some traditions went through reform and accepted
secular law. Others have strong movements that still insist religious law
should rule the state, punish blasphemy, and limit the rights of women and
non-believers. Pretending these differences don't exist doesn't make anyone
safer. It just stops us from talking honestly about which teachings help
people flourish and which hold them back.

The honest position is simple: a religion that respects freedom of conscience
and equal rights is better, by any standard most of us actually live by, than
one that doesn't.

## Saying it out loud

We owe it to the people living under the worst ideas to name them. Dissidents,
women, apostates and minorities inside harsh cultures are the first victims of
our politeness. When we refuse to judge, we side with their oppressors by
default.

Not all cultures are equal. Neither are all religions. Saying so isn't hatred.
It's the beginning of an honest conversation.

## The objections, briefly

In fairness, here are the strongest arguments on the other side:

- **Whose standard?** Critics say "better" always means "more like us," so the
  judgment is circular. Defenders reply that freedom and human dignity are
  near-universal desires, not Western quirks.
- **Cultures aren't monoliths.** Every large culture and religion contains
  huge variety, across countries, eras and individuals. Ranking a whole
  tradition can erase reformers and moderates inside it.
- **Outcomes have many causes.** Colonial history, geography, resources, war
  and economics shape outcomes too. Separating culture's effect from these is
  hard, and the data are often weaker than they look.
- **History moves.** Cultures that look backward today were leaders in other
  centuries, and the reverse. That suggests ideas change, so judgments about
  any tradition aren't permanent.
- **Risk of spillover.** Even critics of relativism warn that "this culture is
  inferior" easily slides into "these people are inferior," which has fueled
  real persecution.
