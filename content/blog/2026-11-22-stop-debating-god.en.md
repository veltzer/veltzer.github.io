Every few months another public debate gets announced: a scientist against a theologian,
an atheist against an apologist, a packed hall, a long video online. Thoughtful people
show up hoping reason will win. My view is that the event itself is the problem. Debating
whether God exists is counterproductive, and anyone who wants to see religion fade should
stop doing it.

## The debate is the concession

When you agree to debate a question, you're saying it's an open one, something reasonable
adults can disagree on. That's the concession, and it's made before anyone says a word.

We don't hold debates on whether Santa Claus exists. Nobody books a hall to argue about
Thor. That's not because no arguments for Thor could be found. A clever person could put
some together. We don't do it because the whole thing would be childish, and treating it
as serious would make the people taking part look ridiculous.

The same goes for Jesus, for Jehovah, and for Allah. The only thing setting them apart
from Thor is how many people believe in them today. Popularity isn't evidence. A big
audience doesn't turn a fairy tale into a philosophical question.

So every time a rationalist walks onto that stage, they lend the other side their
credibility. The believer leaves with a message: serious people take this seriously. Even
if the atheist "wins" on points, religion wins on status.

## Arguments don't change minds

The debate format rests on one assumption: lay out good enough arguments and people will
update their beliefs. That assumption is false.

People aren't logical. They're emotional, social animals. Nobody believes in God because a
syllogism convinced them, and nobody will stop believing because of one. Belief is
[absorbed from parents](@/blog/2026-05-03-geography-of-belief.md), community, and
identity. It's held in place by belonging, comfort, and fear. Logic never touches any of
that.

You can see this in the debates themselves. After decades of them, how many believers
walked out having changed their minds? Each side applauds its own champion and goes home
more sure of itself than before. The arguments the believer brings to the stage
[came after the belief](@/blog/2025-12-24-atheists-are-too-generous.md), as
fortifications, not as its foundation.

## What actually works: social attitude

If arguments don't move people, what does? The same thing that moves them on everything
else: social signals. What respected people treat as normal, and what they treat as
embarrassing.

Beliefs survive when they're respectable. They fade when they become slightly
embarrassing to admit. Astrology, faith healing, belief in witches: none of these went
away because someone won a formal debate. They went from mainstream to fringe as educated
people stopped treating them as worth discussing.

That means the right attitude toward religious belief is dismissal, a kind of open
intellectual snobbery. Not engagement, not careful respect, and not "let's hear both
sides."

## This applies in person too

This isn't only about public stages. It matters most in everyday life.

When someone tells me they believe in God, at least in the Abrahamic sense, I shouldn't
answer with polite interest as if they'd told me something deep. And I shouldn't launch
into a counterargument, because that just restages the debate on a small scale. The
honest response is the one I'd give an adult who told me they believe in Santa Claus:
open disrespect for the belief.

This isn't about cruelty to the person. It's about refusing to pretend. Reflexive respect
for faith is the habit that keeps religion socially protected, and every polite nod
reinforces it. Withdrawing that respect sends a signal people actually understand,
because it reaches them on the emotional, social level where beliefs really live.
[Being entitled to a belief](@/blog/2016-02-28-entitled-to-your-beliefs.md) never meant
being entitled to have it respected.

## Answering the objections

**"But debates have worked before."** They've had three thousand years to prove it.
Philosophers, theologians, and skeptics have argued about God since antiquity, and
religion is still here. A strategy that hasn't delivered in three millennia doesn't
deserve another round.

**"Debates aren't for the believer on stage, they're for the undecided in the
audience."** The undecided are people too, and they don't decide on logic either. They go
by who seems confident, who seems respected, and which side they'd rather belong to. A
debate that treats God as a serious question tells them exactly what they shouldn't hear:
that it is one.

**"Contempt just makes believers dig in."** In the short run, yes. Nobody gives up a
belief the moment it's mocked. But beliefs aren't decided in a moment. Over years, when
believers look around and see that the intelligent people they admire don't respect those
beliefs, the ground shifts. Being out of step with the people you look up to is a
pressure no argument can match. That's how belief changes in the long run. Rude, public
unbelief has a record of
[civilizing religions](@/blog/2011-04-17-atheism-civilizes-religions.md), not hardening
them.

## The point

If the goal is a world less held back by religion, the strategy is clear. Stop giving it
a stage. Stop treating it as a serious intellectual position. Treat it the way we already
treat every other myth we've grown out of.

We don't debate childish beliefs. We outgrow them, and we help others do the same by
showing we already have.
