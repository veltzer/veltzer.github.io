*What flat earthers taught me about religion.*

For years one question bothered me: why are religious people willing to lie in the name of their faith? Not be mistaken. Lie. Deny what is written on the page in front of them, twist facts, wave away evidence, invent excuses that they would laugh at if anyone else used them.

I'm a scientific person by nature, and I simply couldn't understand it. How does a person look at a plain fact and say the opposite?

Social media answered the question for me. And the answer has nothing to do with religion.

## What social media showed us

Social media has been with us for about twenty years now, and if those twenty years have taught us one thing about human nature, it's this: people bow their logic to their group and to their identity. Watch people argue online for any length of time and it becomes impossible to miss.

I don't mean that people reason their way to whatever conclusion suits their group. That would at least be reasoning. I mean that when the group's interest or a person's sense of who they are is at stake, logic is simply switched off. People will spread lies, spread innuendo, and make terrible, plainly counterfactual claims, as long as it protects their group and their way of thinking. The emotional side wins and the logical side is told to sit down.

Yes, occasionally someone changes their mind. It happens so rarely that it isn't worth discussing.

## The sun that wouldn't set

What made it click for me was flat earthers.

Someone filmed the sun over a full 24 hours from near the pole. The sun circles the horizon and never sets. On a flat earth this is impossible. It's about as clean a refutation as you can put on video.

The responses were exactly what you'd guess. The video is fake. The video was stitched together from clips shot at different times, so it isn't really a 24-hour cycle. And my favorite: I don't care, I still believe in a flat earth. Look up flat earthers' responses to the midnight sun yourself and you'll find more variations. Every single one of them dishonest.

In December 2024 a group even flew flat earthers to Antarctica to watch the midnight sun in person, an event called The Final Experiment. Online, people answered the footage by claiming it was really filmed in the Arctic, or by attacking the arrogance of the people laughing at them. Even among those who stood there and watched the sun not set, admissions were partial at best: you could be wrong about this one detail, but not about the flat earth itself.

What struck me most was the blatant denial of facts. And here is the point: there is no church here, no holy book, no priesthood. Just a group, and an identity. And the behavior is identical to what I'd seen from religious people for years.

So it isn't religion. It's who you identify yourself as, and your loyalty to your group. Religion is just the place where that loyalty is oldest and runs deepest.

Which brings us back to the faithful. They are not any better.

## The Bible and slavery

Take slavery. The Bible accepts it, and it is not subtle about it.

Leviticus 25 lets the Israelites buy slaves from the surrounding nations, hold them as property, and pass them on to their children as an inheritance. Exodus 21 regulates the beating of slaves, and lets a master who beats his slave go unpunished if the slave survives a day or two, because the slave is his property. In the New Testament, Ephesians 6 tells slaves to obey their masters. These are words on a page. They are plain and understandable. There is no room for interpretation.

And the slavery in question was not gentle. Relations between people in the 7th or 5th century BCE were far more violent than today. Capital punishment was everywhere, women were treated harshly, and war, while small in scale, was savage. My own reasonable inference is that the actual relationship between a master and a slave back then was more brutal than it was even in the Atlantic slave trade, terrible as that trade was in its scale. I'm not saying the Hebrews were worse than their neighbors. They were almost certainly the norm for their time. That's exactly the point: they had slavery, it was ugly, and their scripture reflects it.

And still, I've seen a million versions of the same denial. It wasn't really slavery, it was indentured servitude. The Bible only regulated slavery, it never endorsed it. In my experience, nearly every Christian I've seen address these passages has denied what they say. They aren't confused about the text. The text is clear. They are protecting something more important to them than the text.

## The engineer and the slowed-down atoms

This one is personal.

I had a friend, an engineer, religious, who believed in a young earth. When I brought up the evidence that the earth is about 4.5 billion years old, he had an answer: God could have slowed down all subatomic interactions, so that the earth merely looks billions of years old while actually being young.

This is an engineer. A man trained to respect measurement and evidence.

He wasn't the first. In 1857 the naturalist Philip Gosse argued, in a book called *Omphalos*, that God created the world already looking old: fossils, rock layers and all. The beauty of the argument, from the believer's side, is that no possible evidence can ever touch it. Every observation that contradicts the belief becomes part of the illusion.

In my eyes this is the epitome of dishonesty. It isn't an argument. It's a decision, made in advance, that nothing will be allowed to count.

## "You can't prove it didn't happen"

Jews aren't any better.

The Exodus, as described, did not happen. Most archaeologists, including Israeli archaeologists like Israel Finkelstein, have concluded as much. Two million people wandering in Sinai for forty years would leave traces. There are none. Egypt left extensive records, and none of them mention a mass escape of slaves, the plagues, or the loss of an army in the sea.

And yet no religious Jew I know will accept this evidence. The answer is always the same: you can't prove it didn't happen.

It's the same move as the engineer's slowed-down atoms. The burden of proof is flipped, so nothing could ever count against the belief. When people do go looking for supporting evidence, it gets worse. Ron Wyatt claimed to have found Pharaoh's chariot wheels on the floor of the Red Sea, and those claims circulated for years without anything behind them.

## Who wrote the Torah

Orthodox Judaism holds that Moses wrote the five books of the Torah. People like to point out that the Torah describes Moses's own death and burial. That never impressed me much. The real evidence is much stronger.

The Torah tells many of its stories twice. There are two creation stories in Genesis 1 and 2, with things created in a different order. The flood story is two versions woven together: in one, Noah takes two of every animal; in the other, seven pairs of the clean ones. No single author writes like that.

The styles and vocabularies differ from section to section, and the text alternates between divine names, YHWH and Elohim, with each name tracking its own style. There are anachronisms too. Genesis 36 lists the kings of Edom who reigned before any king ruled over Israel, something only a writer living after Israel had kings would think to say. And camels appear as domesticated animals before they were domesticated in that region.

This is not one man's book. It was assembled from several sources over a long time. And still, the believer insists.

## The split moon

Islamic tradition holds that Muhammad split the moon in two. There is no evidence that it happened, and from a physical standpoint it is an impossibility. A falsehood, protected by believers.

It has its own version of the flat earther's trick, run in reverse. Some believers passed around NASA photos of a long groove on the moon's surface, a lunar rille, as proof of the split. NASA scientist Brad Bailey said plainly that there is no evidence the moon was ever split. If the evidence doesn't support the belief, you misread it until it does.

## When the date comes and goes

If you want evidence as plain as it gets, try a prophecy with a date on it.

The Millerites announced that Christ would return on October 22, 1844. The day came, and nothing happened. Many did not abandon the belief. They reinterpreted the prophecy as a heavenly event nobody could see, and out of that movement grew the Seventh-day Adventists. Jehovah's Witnesses went through their own versions of this around 1914, 1925, and 1975.

The psychologist Leon Festinger watched this happen up close. For his 1956 book *When Prophecy Fails*, he and his colleagues infiltrated a small UFO group whose prophecy of the end of the world failed on schedule. Many members didn't give up. They doubled down and began recruiting harder.

That is my whole thesis, observed by a scientist seventy years ago. Identity and the group beat the facts.

## More of the same

The list goes on. When carbon dating put the Shroud of Turin in the Middle Ages, defenders said the samples were contaminated or taken from a medieval repair. Young-earth creationists reject radiometric dating, distant starlight, and the fossil record. Some Muslim apologists find modern embryology and the Big Bang in vague verses, but only after science discovered them. Some Hindus insist that a natural chain of sand and coral shoals is the bridge Rama's army built. Faith healers are caught red-handed and their followers keep paying.

Different religions, different countries, different centuries. The same behavior.

## A note on fairness

I'm skeptical of every religion on this point, and I've tried to draw my examples from several of them. That doesn't mean I think all religions are equally harmful. They aren't. But harm is a different question from honesty, and a subject for another post.

## Why the faithful lie

So why do the faithful lie? Not because religion turns people into liars. They lie because they are human, and humans will bend or abandon logic to protect their group and their sense of who they are. When a belief is part of your identity, an attack on the belief feels like an attack on you, and the facts lose.

For most of history this was hard to see clearly. Religion was the obvious example, so it was easy to think religion was the cause. Social media changed that. Now we watch people do it every day, about politics, about sports teams, about the shape of the earth. Once you've seen a flat earther look at the sun circling the horizon and say "I don't care," a believer saying "you can't prove it didn't happen" no longer looks mysterious.

It looks human.
