For centuries, religious traditions have placed their foundational scriptures on an
unassailable aesthetic pedestal. These texts are celebrated not merely as theological
guides but as pinnacle achievements of human expression. Strip away the theology,
though, and judge them the way we judge any other book - on narrative craft, character,
structure, and the ability to hold a reader who owes them nothing - and the reputation
collapses quickly.

## The Parochial Readership Problem

A basic test of great literature is whether it survives the departure of its original
audience. The Homeric epics, Shakespeare, Dostoevsky, Kafka - these are read across
geography and ideology purely for narrative mastery and thematic depth. A secular
reader needs no faith in Mount Olympus to be moved by the *Iliad*.

Sacred texts fail this test badly. Outside ritual obligation and community enforcement,
foundational religious books are almost never read for pleasure. A Christian does not
pick up the Quran on holiday; a Muslim does not turn to the Book of Mormon for aesthetic
enrichment. Reading holy texts is driven overwhelmingly by duty. If these books had
genuine literary genius, they would thrive in the open market of secular fiction, the
way *Gilgamesh* and the Greek myths still do. Their inability to attract cross-cultural
readers on aesthetic grounds alone suggests their perceived value is an artifact of
religious socialization rather than narrative excellence.

## Texts Written for Prescientific Audiences

Foundational scriptures reflect the cognitive and material environment in which they
were compiled. Produced in prescientific tribal societies, their narrative logic leans
on proclamation, repetition, and immediate external causality rather than on ambiguity
or psychological development.

- **Flat characters.** The narratives are populated by archetypes with minimal
  interiority. Characters rarely change their minds for reasons the text bothers to
  explore.
- **Regional horizons.** The worldview is bounded by local geography, agrarian
  concerns, and tribal conflict. There is no sense of planetary scale or systemic
  social force.
- **Authority over argument.** The prose relies on fear, divine retribution, and
  obedience rather than on reasoned ethical inquiry or persuasion.

## The Primitive Mechanics of Miracles

A good indicator of narrative sophistication is how a text resolves conflict. In
serious fiction, resolution comes from character action, systemic friction, or
consequence. In holy texts, progression relies almost entirely on magical intervention.

The central miracles - seas parting, animals talking, bushes burning without being
consumed, food falling from the sky - belong aesthetically to elementary folklore.
They are transparent *deus ex machina* devices designed to produce awe in an audience
with no naturalistic grasp of meteorology, biology, or physics. Read without
theological deference, these episodes reduce cosmic stakes to the logic of a fairy
tale. Modern fantasy authors are held to a much higher standard: invent magic if you
like, but give it rules, costs, and consequences. Scripture is exempted from that
standard only because we have agreed in advance not to apply it.

## Enduring Literature Versus Scriptural Canon

| Criterion | Enduring masterpieces | Traditional holy books |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Audience | Read across cultures and eras for enjoyment and insight | Read almost exclusively within the in-group, out of duty |
| Character | Multidimensional figures with internal conflict and change | Flat archetypes governed by obedience, fear, or zeal |
| Resolution | Grounded in choices, complexity, and realistic causality | Dependent on supernatural intervention and folktale tropes |
| Structure | Deliberate thematic architecture and stylistic cohesion | Fragmented compilations with conflicting chronologies |

## The Illusion of a Complete Framework

Apologists frequently argue that religious texts supply a comprehensive legal, ethical,
and existential framework. On inspection, that framework is largely retrofitted.

1. **Decrees, not principles.** Moral instructions rarely establish foundational
   reasoning such as reciprocity, harm reduction, or rights. They substitute raw
   command.
2. **Structural incoherence.** Most scriptures are composite works edited over
   centuries by multiple anonymous hands, so they lack internal consistency and require
   centuries of secondary apologetics to manufacture it.
3. **Completeness after the fact.** The impression of a complete life framework exists
   because institutions imposed systematic interpretation retroactively onto texts that
   are inherently fragmented.

## The Honest Version of the Claim

None of this means the texts are worthless. They are historically indispensable, they
contain individual passages of real power, and their influence on later literature is
enormous - you cannot read Milton or Dostoevsky or Faulkner without them. But
"historically important and occasionally beautiful" is a very different claim from
"the greatest book ever written."

Removed from the protective sphere of reverence and subjected to ordinary literary
analysis, sacred texts read as what they are: anthologies of an ancient era, valuable
as artifacts, constrained by the narrative and intellectual horizons of the societies
that produced them. Their exalted literary reputation is largely unearned, and it
survives mainly because so few people are willing to say so out loud.
