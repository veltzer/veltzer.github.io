I have argued before that [truth is long and stories are short](@/blog/brevity_is_not_truth.en.md), and that this is why falsehood travels better than fact. Here I want to make a narrower and less forgivable accusation. Art does not merely fail to teach us how to think. **It actively trains a way of thinking that is wrong for almost every question that matters.**

## The Unit of Art Is the Individual

A novel follows one person, or a handful. A film has a protagonist. A painting has a face, or a scene with a few faces in it. This is not a limitation that artists struggle against; it is the medium. Art works by making you feel what one particular person feels, and it cannot do that for a million people at once.

But almost nothing about how a society works is visible at the scale of one person. Whether a policy helps or harms, whether a war was necessary, whether a group is being treated unjustly, whether an economy is improving — these are questions about distributions, about averages and variances and tails. The individual case is one data point, and one data point tells you almost nothing.

So the habit art installs — understand the world by inhabiting a single case — is precisely the habit that fails when you turn to any question about the public rather than the private.

## A Story Proves Existence, Not Significance

Here is the specific error, and it is worth being exact about it because it is so common.

A journalist finds one person harmed by a policy and tells their story with skill. The story proves that the harm exists. It does not prove that the harm is large, or common, or larger than the harm the policy prevents. Existence is the weakest possible claim about a phenomenon; it is satisfied by a single instance. Significance is a claim about how many instances there are relative to everything else, and no story, however moving, can carry that information.

**The line between good thinking and bad thinking runs exactly here**, between "this happens" and "this matters," and art lives permanently on the wrong side of it. Bad journalism does the same thing for the same reason: it is art with a deadline.

Notice that the story is not lying. Every detail may be true. The falsehood is in the inference the audience is invited to draw, and the invitation is the whole craft.

## Emotion Instead of Coolness

Art's second lesson is that the right response to a situation is to feel it. Symbols, resonance, the sense of a larger meaning — these are what a work of art is trying to produce, and a work that leaves you unmoved has failed on its own terms.

But the questions I listed above are answered better cold than hot. Emotion is what makes you grab the nearest ideology, the framework that tells you which side the story is on before you have looked at the numbers. Art rewards exactly that reflex: the satisfying story is the one where the symbols line up and the villain is who you expected.

I do not think it is an accident that artists were so taken with Nietzsche. He is the philosopher of the singular heroic individual against the herd, and that is the story art already knows how to tell. He gave the medium's built-in bias a philosophy, and the medium was grateful. The real story of any century is the story of the masses — what they ate, what killed them, what they could afford — and it has almost no heroes in it.

## Whose Story Art Has Always Told

There is a historical point that makes the epistemic one sharper.

For most of history artists worked for whoever could pay them, and that meant the powerful. The court painter painted the king. The poet praised the patron. The cathedral was commissioned by the bishop and glorified the bishop's god and the bishop's donors. The poor appear in this record as background, as picturesque detail, or not at all.

We already know to treat history books written before the twentieth century with suspicion, because we know who wrote them and for whom. **The same suspicion should apply by default to every work of historical art**, and for the same reason. It is the powerful describing the world from the point of view of the powerful, in a form designed to move rather than to inform. That it is beautiful does not make it a source.

## What I Am Not Saying

I am not saying art has no value. Pleasure is a value, and so is consolation, and so is the sheer craft of a thing well made. I would not want to live without any of them.

I am saying that art is a bad instrument for understanding the world, that it is routinely used as one, and that the way it fails is not random. It fails toward the individual over the population, toward feeling over measurement, toward the symbol over the case, and toward the patron over the crowd. Those are four systematic biases, all pointing the same way, and a culture that takes its understanding of itself from its art will be wrong in all four directions at once.
