Direct democracy is usually presented as the radical proposal and representative government as the sober default. I want to reverse the framing, because I think the evolutionary facts run the other way. Deciding things together is what humans did for almost the whole of their existence. Handing the decisions to a few hundred strangers and checking back every four years is the novelty, and a very recent one.

## The Environment We Were Built For

For the overwhelming majority of the time our species has existed, humans lived in small bands. Estimates vary, but the number that keeps coming up is a few dozen people, everyone known to everyone.

In a group that size, decisions that affected the group were made by the group. Not by a vote with ballots, but by talk: where to move, whom to trust, how to divide the kill, what to do about the member who kept taking more than his share. Everyone who would live with the consequences had a say in the decision, and everyone could see how the decision was made.

There were leaders, but they were not rulers. A hunter whose judgement was trusted led the hunt. An elder whose memory was long was listened to on matters of memory. Influence tracked competence and reputation, and it was revocable the moment either lapsed, because the people revoking it were standing right there.

**That is the arrangement our social instincts were shaped for.** Whatever else is true of human nature, it includes an expectation of participating in the decisions of one's community, and a sensitivity to being excluded from them.

## What We Have Instead

Now look at the arrangement we actually live under.

Decisions about war, taxation, the law, the money, what children are taught, what may be built and where, are made by a few hundred people whom almost nobody has met. The individual's connection to any of it is one vote, cast every few years, for a person rather than for a decision, from a menu of two or three.

Beyond that vote, the individual's responsibility to the community has been reduced to nearly nothing. What remains is commercial: you pay taxes, you buy things, you obey. Responsibility has been outsourced to institutions and paid for by transaction. That is a real arrangement and it has real advantages, but let us not pretend it is satisfying. Nobody has ever felt part of a community because they filed a return.

## The Evidence That Something Is Missing

If participation were merely a nice-to-have, people would not bother. They do bother, constantly and at real cost.

They march. They sign petitions that nobody reads. They argue about policy with strangers on the internet for hours, unpaid, on questions over which they have no influence whatever. They join parties, attend meetings, donate to campaigns, and write to representatives who will never see the letter.

And they are frustrated. Ask almost anyone about politics and what you hear is not indifference but a specific complaint: nobody listens, nothing changes, it does not matter what I think.

Put those two facts together. **People spend enormous effort trying to influence their community and report, almost universally, that the effort does not work.** That is exactly the pattern you would expect from an instinct that is being exercised against a structure that does not accept its input. The urge is intact; the outlet is missing.

I think a substantial part of what is called political frustration is this, and nothing more complicated: a biological need to participate in the decisions of one's group, in a society that has arranged for that participation to be almost entirely ceremonial.

## The Objection From Scale

The reply is obvious. We do not live in bands of forty. We live in nations of millions, and the band's way of deciding does not scale.

Granted. Direct democracy at the level of a state is a very long way from a fire circle, and I am not claiming otherwise. Millions of people cannot talk a question through the way forty can.

But notice what the objection actually establishes. It establishes that the *mechanism* must change with scale. It says nothing about whether the *function* should be abandoned. We did not respond to the scale problem in medicine by deciding that people no longer need to be healed, only that healing had to be organised differently. The need remains; the delivery changes.

Direct democracy is the attempt to keep the function while changing the mechanism. It gives millions of people a way to do, on the questions that matter most, the thing forty people did around the fire: hear the case, weigh it, and decide, with the decision being theirs. It is a distant descendant of the band's arrangement. Representation is not a descendant of it at all. Representation is what you get when the function is dropped and only the ceremony is kept.

## Why This Matters for the Argument

I am not saying that because something is natural it is good. Plenty of natural things are terrible, and I have argued elsewhere that we should sometimes [push deliberately against our instincts](@/blog/forcing_people_against_nature.en.md).

The point is narrower. When direct democracy is called radical and representation is called normal, a claim is being smuggled in about which one is the departure from the human baseline. That claim is false. The strange arrangement, the one that requires justification, is the one where a community of millions has almost no say in its own affairs and is told this is what freedom looks like. Direct democracy does not need to explain why people should want to decide things. It needs only to explain how, and that is an engineering question rather than a question about human nature.
