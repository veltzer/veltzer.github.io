We are frequently told to treat religious texts as literary masterpieces, speaking of them in hushed, sacred tones. Phrases like "the greatest story ever told" are treated as established consensus, and even secular biblical scholars like Bart Ehrman often praise concepts like the "genius of Mark."

Yet when evaluated on objective literary merits, there is no inherent genius to be found. The fundamental mechanism driving the perceived greatness of religious literature is audience confirmation bias. People deem a work of literature "great" primarily when it validates assumptions, emotional desires, and theological biases they already hold.

When an individual desires to believe in a benevolent, supreme deity for which no empirical evidence exists, a narrative that affirms "God exists, and He loves you" is inevitably judged to be profound and high-quality. This psychological mechanism also explains why believers universally praise their own scripture as sublime while dismissing the sacred stories of competing religions as crude, absurd, or low-quality. The evaluation of literary quality in religious contexts is entirely compromised by bias.

To properly evaluate religious narratives, one must look at how they are received by individuals stripped of faith — those who do not accept the supernatural claims or believe in the divine authority of the texts. Without the emotional buffer of confirmation bias, the facade collapses: the narratives emerge as poorly constructed, repetitive, primitive, and logically incoherent. In short, stripped of belief, the story is not a masterpiece; it is a piece of shit.
