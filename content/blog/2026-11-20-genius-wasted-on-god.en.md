Intelligence doesn't protect you from nonsense. Some of the greatest minds in
history spent years of their lives doing abysmal work for one purpose: to prop
up their belief in God. The lesson isn't that they were stupid. It's that no
amount of brilliance survives a conclusion you've already decided to reach.

## Newton: the greatest scientist, decoding the Bible

Isaac Newton gave us the laws of motion, universal gravitation, and calculus.
He also wrote more about theology and alchemy than about physics, by most
estimates more than a million words. He combed the books of Daniel and
Revelation for hidden codes, reconstructed the dimensions of Solomon's Temple
as if they held cosmic secrets, and calculated from biblical prophecy that the
world wouldn't end before 2060. He spent decades trying to turn base metals
into gold.

The same man who demanded mathematical proof for the motion of planets
accepted ancient texts as a code from God, with no evidence that there was any
code at all. The method that made him great simply switched off.

## Gödel: a proof about undefined words

Kurt Gödel proved the incompleteness theorems, one of the deepest results in
the history of mathematics. Late in life he wrote a formal proof that God
exists, using modal logic. The logic is valid; computers have checked it. But
it rests on "positive properties" that Gödel never defines, and on treating
necessary existence as one of them. A valid proof built on undefined axioms
proves nothing about the world; [logic cannot bring anything into
existence](@/blog/2014-07-19-logic-cannot-prove-existence.md). The man who
showed the limits of formal systems tried to squeeze God out of one.

## Descartes: God as the guarantee of knowledge

René Descartes set out to doubt everything and rebuild knowledge from
certainty. Then, to escape his own doubt, he reached for God. He argued that
the idea of a perfect being must have been put in his mind by a perfect being,
and that since God isn't a deceiver, our senses can be trusted. The whole
foundation of modern philosophy rests on an argument that assumes what it sets
out to prove.

## Cantor: infinity as revelation

Georg Cantor founded set theory and showed that some infinities are larger
than others, a genuine revolution. Then he spent years of his working life on
theology. He believed his transfinite numbers had been revealed to him by God,
corresponded at length with Catholic theologians and cardinals, and wrote to
convince the Church that his mathematics fit its doctrine of the infinite.
None of it advanced his mathematics by a single theorem. It was time a
once-in-a-century mind could have spent on the open problems he had created,
including the continuum hypothesis, which he never resolved.

## Kepler: planets arranged by divine geometry

Johannes Kepler discovered the laws of planetary motion. Before that, he spent
years convinced that God had spaced the planets according to the five Platonic
solids nested inside each other. It was beautiful, it was theological, and it
was wrong. To his credit, when the data from Tycho Brahe's observations didn't
fit, Kepler eventually followed the data, and that's exactly why he's
remembered.

## Why smart people do this

Intelligence is a tool for reaching conclusions, not for choosing them. When a
brilliant person starts from a belief they won't give up, their intelligence
doesn't free them from it. It makes them better at defending it. They build
more elaborate codes, more rigorous-looking proofs, and more ingenious
arguments, all in service of a conclusion fixed in advance. A clever person
can [rationalise anything](@/blog/2019-08-30-justification-and-bias.md).

The pattern is the same every time. Where these people let reality push back,
through measurement, data, and proof, they did immortal work. Where they let
faith set the destination, they wasted their genius.

## The other side

Defenders would say this is unfair. Religious belief was nearly universal in
Newton's and Kepler's time, and for many of them faith was the motivation for
science itself: they studied nature to understand the mind of God. Historians
also point out that alchemy was the chemistry of its day and not obviously
foolish at the time. And some philosophers still take ontological arguments
seriously as logic puzzles, even if they reject the conclusion.
