Say the name Karl Marx and most people picture gulags, bread lines, and giant
portraits hanging over military parades. That's understandable, since regimes
that called themselves Marxist ran some of the worst states of the twentieth
century. But it's also unfair. To my mind Marx is one of the sharpest critics
capitalism ever had, and one of the worst guides to designing a government.
The trouble is that we've mixed the two up.

## The diagnosis was good

Marx's real work was analysis. He spent decades studying how industrial
capitalism actually behaved, and much of what he described still holds:

- **Boom and bust.** He argued that capitalism produces recurring crises of
  overproduction and speculation. Anyone who lived through 2008 knows the
  pattern.
- **Concentration.** He predicted capital would gather into fewer and larger
  firms. Look at any industry dominated by three or four giants.
- **Labour as a commodity.** He described how the market treats a worker's
  time as an input to be bought as cheaply as possible, and how that shapes
  people's lives.
- **Globalisation.** The Communist Manifesto describes capital spreading
  across every border and dissolving local industries and traditions. That
  reads more like 1995 than 1848.

You don't have to be a revolutionary to find this useful. Economists,
historians, and sociologists who would never call themselves Marxists borrow
these tools all the time.

## The prescription was empty

This is where Marx falls apart. Read the Federalist Papers and you get 85
essays of careful institutional engineering: two-year terms for the House and
six for the Senate, staggered elections, an independent judiciary,
appointments that need confirmation, a veto and an override, and power divided
between states and the federal government. Madison and Hamilton argued over
numbers, mechanisms, and incentives because they knew that's where freedom is
won or lost.

Marx did none of that work. His model for the future state comes to a few
pages of praise for the Paris Commune, a single city that lasted about two
months. Elected delegates who could be recalled at any time and were paid
workers' wages may sound democratic, but there's no separation of powers, no
courts that can check the majority, and no protection for minorities. He also
deliberately refused to write blueprints and dismissed them as utopian
speculation.

Scale makes it worse. What works in a family, a small band, or a single city
doesn't carry over to a country of tens or hundreds of millions. Madison
understood this and built for it. In Federalist No. 10 he argued that a large
republic is safer precisely because many competing factions make it hard for
any one of them to take over. Marx never seriously took on the problem.

## He misread human nature

Underneath the missing machinery is a deeper mistake. Marx believed that greed
and the abuse of power came mainly from class conflict, so that once classes
disappeared, the state would "wither away." The framers assumed the opposite:
people will always seek power, so the system has to set ambition against
ambition. History settled that argument. Every state built on Marx's vague
political ideas filled the vacuum with a party, then a leader, and nothing
could check either one.

## Class matters less than he thought

Even his analysis has limits. Marx expected class to become the main dividing
line in society. Instead, nation, religion, and ethnicity often proved
stronger, as in 1914, when workers marched off to kill each other for their
countries. A large middle class also blurred his clean split between workers
and owners. Class still shapes health, education, and life chances, but it
isn't the master key he believed it was.

## So why the bad rap?

Because we judge Marx by Lenin, Stalin, and Mao, people who took his critique,
filled his empty political blueprint with their own authoritarian designs, and
put his name on the result. It's a bit like blaming a doctor who correctly
diagnosed a disease for the quack who later prescribed poison.

The fair verdict is a split one. Read Marx to understand what's wrong with
capitalism. For how to govern, read Madison.

## The other side

Marx's defenders would push back on part of this. Some argue that he left
blueprints out on purpose, because he thought future generations should design
their own institutions. Others say the Soviet model distorted his ideas so
badly that it tells us little about him. Critics from the other direction
argue that the authoritarian outcome wasn't an accident: a theory that
promises to abolish politics leaves no legitimate place for opposition, so the
road to one-party rule was built into the ideas themselves. And many
economists would say capitalism has adapted, through welfare states, central
banks, and regulation, in ways that answered many of the problems Marx
described without overthrowing anything.
