"Morality" is a word we stretch across too many contexts, so let me make a narrow claim and defend only that: **direct democracy would produce more moral outcomes than representative government — not because its participants are better people, but because of the shape of the system.**

## The Argument

Look at the situations we most readily and most confidently call immoral. Not the contested cases in ethics seminars, but the ones nobody argues about: a company poisoning a water supply and concealing it, a government imprisoning critics, an industry capturing the regulator meant to constrain it, a firm selling arms to a regime conducting a massacre.

They share a feature. **In each case a minority is using concentrated power to extract from, or impose on, a majority that cannot stop it.**

The concentration is not incidental. It is what makes the act possible and what makes it wrong. Remove it and the case dissolves: a company that could not conceal the poisoning could not poison; a government that could not silence critics is merely a government that is criticised.

If concentration is the common factor across the clearest cases of immorality, then dispersing power is not one policy among others. It is an attack on the precondition.

## Why This Is Not the Usual Argument

The usual case for democracy is about consent or representation: people should have a say in what is done to them. That is a fine argument but a different one, and it is not what I am making.

My claim is causal rather than procedural. **Concentrated power produces immoral outcomes at a higher rate**, regardless of who holds it, because concentration removes the feedback that would otherwise correct the behaviour. The person harmed cannot retaliate, cannot exit, cannot appeal to anyone with an interest in listening. That is what having power over someone *means*.

So dispersal is not valuable because it is fair. It is valuable because it restores the corrective mechanism.

## The Consequence Worth Sitting With

This makes morality a property of a system's *shape* rather than of the virtue of the people inside it.

That is an uncomfortable conclusion, and it should be. It means that replacing bad actors with good ones — the reflexive response to every institutional scandal — addresses almost nothing. Put a person of exemplary character into a position of concentrated, unaccountable power and the outcomes improve somewhat and temporarily. The position still lacks the feedback that keeps behaviour honest, and the next occupant may be ordinary.

It also means the reverse: **you do not need virtuous participants to get moral outcomes.** You need a configuration in which harming others is difficult, visible, and costly to the person doing it. Ordinary self-interested people in such a configuration behave better than saints in a badly shaped one.

## What Follows for Direct Democracy

Representative government concentrates decision-making in a few thousand people, who are then subject to lobbying by a few thousand more. Whatever its merits, it is structurally the arrangement I have just described as the precondition for the outcomes we call immoral.

Direct democracy disperses that decision-making across the whole population. This does not make the population wise, and I am not claiming it does. Publics vote for foolish things regularly.

But it dismantles the specific structure that lets a small group impose on a large one — because there is no longer a small group holding the lever, and therefore no small group worth capturing. **The lobbying problem does not get solved; it gets made pointless**, since there is nobody whose purchase would deliver the outcome.

That is a lower bar than "wise government." It is also a more achievable one, and if the argument above is right, it addresses the thing that actually generates the harms.
