In 1979 the sociologist Alvin Gouldner published a short, strange, and mostly forgotten book called *The Future of Intellectuals and the Rise of the New Class*. Its thesis was that a new social class had appeared in the industrial world: not the owners of capital and not the workers, but the people whose capital is their education. Engineers, scientists, technicians, analysts, teachers, the whole population that lives by knowing things and saying them clearly.

Gouldner made two observations about this class that I think were correct and have become more correct since.

## The Culture of Critical Discourse

The first is that the new class has a shared culture, and the culture is a way of talking. Gouldner called it the culture of critical discourse. Its rule is that a claim is to be justified by argument and evidence, not by the rank of the person making it. You do not get to be right because you are the boss, the bishop, or the elder. You get to be right by showing your work.

Anyone who has worked among engineers recognises this immediately. It is the ordinary etiquette of a code review or a lab meeting. A junior person who is correct beats a senior person who is not, and everyone in the room understands that this is how it should be, even when the senior person sulks.

**That etiquette is politically explosive**, because almost nothing in the surrounding political order runs on it. Parties run on loyalty. Governments run on hierarchy. Public debate runs on who has the microphone. A class trained to demand justification for every claim is a class that finds the entire apparatus of representative politics faintly ridiculous, and says so at dinner.

## The Class That Sees the Closed Doors

The second observation follows from the first. The new class is unusually well placed to notice that many parts of society are not open to argument at all, and that not everything is on the table in a democracy.

Its members work inside the machinery. They see how decisions are actually reached in the corporation, the ministry, the hospital, and they know how far it is from the story told outside. They are trained to ask why, and they keep asking after the point where a polite citizen would stop. So they notice the questions that never come up for a vote, the assumptions that no party questions, the doors that are closed to the discourse they were raised in.

Gouldner saw this as the basis for a political role the class had never managed to play. It knew what was wrong. It had the habits of mind to describe what was wrong. And it did nothing much about it.

## Why It Never Acted

The reason was size and dispersion.

The new class was large in aggregate but scattered across every institution and every city. It had no factory floor, no parish, no meeting hall. The industrial working class could organise because it was physically gathered by the process of production; a thousand men in one building can find each other. A thousand engineers in a thousand offices cannot. Whatever they had in common, they had nowhere to discover that they had it.

So the class that was, by training, the least deferential to power was, by geography, the least able to coordinate against it. It stayed a category in a sociology book rather than an actor in politics.

## The Room It Was Missing

Gouldner died in 1980, so he never saw the thing that removed his objection.

**The internet is the meeting hall the new class never had.** It is the first medium in which people scattered across every institution can find each other by the content of what they say rather than by where they happen to stand. And the culture of the medium, in its better corners, is exactly the culture Gouldner described: show your sources, state your argument, expect to be corrected by a stranger with a better one.

Look at who actually populates the long-form political discussion online. Not the industrial working class, whose numbers have shrunk, and not the owners of capital, who have no need of it. It is overwhelmingly the class Gouldner identified, arguing among itself about how society is run, in a register that assumes rank is not an argument.

That is not yet political action. It is the discussion that precedes it. But the discussion was the part that could never happen before, because the participants could never assemble. Now they assemble constantly, and what they are discussing, whether or not they use the words, is the gap between how decisions are supposed to be made and how they are.

## Why This Matters for the System

If a class trained in critical discourse is, for the first time, able to talk to itself at scale, then the thing least likely to survive the conversation is any arrangement that depends on not being examined.

Representative politics is such an arrangement. Its justifications are the sort that hold up while nobody presses, and the new class presses by reflex. I do not know what it will build instead, and I doubt it knows either. But the class that was ripe for political action for half a century and lacked only a room has been given one, and I would not bet on the closed doors staying closed.
