That a left/right binary distorts individual thinking is a familiar complaint. People adopt a side, then adopt its full package of positions, then defend positions they never examined because their side holds them. This is well documented and widely lamented.

The less discussed damage is what the binary does to *institutions* — and that damage is worse, because institutions are supposed to be the thing that corrects individuals.

## A Case I Keep Returning To

Thomas Sowell's economics writing deals almost exclusively with the failures of government. Read it straight through and you would conclude that government is the source of essentially every economic problem, and that markets, whatever their minor imperfections, are not where the trouble lies.

Sowell is a serious economist and the analysis of government failure is often excellent. But the *distribution* of his attention is strange for someone examining an economy. Market failures exist. Corporate mismanagement exists, monopoly power exists, regulatory capture exists, and firms bribing officials exists. These get a fraction of the treatment that public-sector failure receives.

Sowell was a fellow of the Hoover Institution. Institutions have affiliations. I am not accusing him of writing anything he did not believe — that is the wrong model, and it is why this problem is hard to see. **The question is whether he was free to pursue corporate wrongdoing with the same energy he brought to government wrongdoing**, and to publish the result under that letterhead.

## The Mechanism

Generalise it. A two-party system does not only sort voters. It forces *institutions* to sort: think tanks, research bodies, news outlets, foundations, increasingly universities.

Once an institution has an affiliation, ordinary organisational pressure does the rest. It does not require anyone to lie. It requires only that certain lines of inquiry are less encouraged, certain findings less celebrated, certain hires less likely, and certain conclusions more difficult to publish without a difficult meeting. The individuals inside experience this as a research culture, not as censorship.

**The harm is structural, and locating it in anyone's dishonesty misses it entirely.** Every person in the chain can be acting in good faith and the output is still bent.

## Why This Is Worse Than Biased Voters

Here is the part that matters.

The truth does not have sides. On any complex question, some things the left has right and some things the right has right, and the actual answer is assembled from both — usually in a proportion that satisfies neither.

An individual with a bias is a manageable problem, because institutions exist precisely to correct for individual bias. Peer review, editorial standards, replication, adversarial scrutiny: the whole apparatus is built on the assumption that the institution is more reliable than the person.

**When the institution itself has pre-committed to a side, that correction mechanism runs backwards.** It no longer filters bias out; it enforces bias in, and it does so with the authority that made institutions worth trusting. The reader who checks a claim against a think tank's research is now checking one affiliation against another.

## What It Costs Us

The result is that on the questions where we most need institutions capable of reporting what their own side gets wrong, we have almost none. Each has staked its credibility on a position, and credibility once staked is defended.

So we get two research ecosystems producing two bodies of evidence, each internally rigorous, each incapable of the specific finding that would embarrass its patrons. And a public that correctly senses something is wrong, draws the conclusion that expertise is worthless, and stops listening to anybody.

That last step is the real cost. The two-party system's capture of institutions does not merely produce bad research. **It destroys the possibility of a shared factual basis for argument**, which is the thing a democracy actually runs on.
