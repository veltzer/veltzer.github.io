Social psychology has a name for one of our most reliable mistakes. The *fundamental attribution error* is our tendency to explain what people do by what they are like, rather than by the situation they are standing in. Its close relative, *personality attribution*, is the specific form: we reach for someone's character, knowledge, traits, or temperament, and almost never for the circumstances constraining them.

The error is well documented in personal life. What is much less discussed is what it does to politics — where, I want to argue, it is the single largest obstacle to seeing our system of government clearly.

## The Error in Miniature

Start small. You invite a friend to a game of chess and he declines. Unless he offers a convincing situational reason — he is ill, he has to be somewhere — you conclude, probably correctly, that he did not feel like playing. You have attributed his behaviour to his preferences. That is personality attribution, and in this case it is likely right.

The laboratory versions are more unsettling, because they show the error surviving conditions where it plainly should not.

Take the classic questioner-and-contestant experiment. Split a group of students at random. Half are told to invent hard questions from their own knowledge; the other half must answer them. The questioners, naturally, ask about the things they happen to know, and the answerers do badly. Afterwards, both groups rate the general knowledge of both parties. The questioners rate themselves as roughly average. The answerers rate themselves as *below* average.

This is statistically absurd. The roles were assigned by coin flip. There is no reason to expect any difference in knowledge between the two groups, and the observers know how the roles were handed out. The advantage was entirely structural — one group chose the topic — and yet the participants read it as a fact about the people.

A sharper version removes even the appearance of a real test. Give people a task whose answers they could not possibly know — identifying the sex of the author of a text that contains no clue about it — and then report their scores by flipping a coin. Those told they did well rate their ability higher. Those told they did badly rate it lower.

Then comes the part that should genuinely disturb you. **Tell them the score was a coin flip.** Explain that you have no idea how they actually performed, that the number was random, that the task had no discoverable answer. They continue to believe they would do well, or badly, on such a test. The situational explanation is handed to them, in full, and it does not dislodge the personal one.

That is the strength of the effect we are dealing with. It is not that we lack the information. It is that the information does not help.

## The Error in Politics

Now scale it up.

A demonstration breaks out in Iran. The US Secretary of State appears and voices support for the demonstrators — not full-throated support, but support. Ask people why she said it, including journalists who follow politics for a living, and you will hear that it was her decision. Or the President's. A choice, reflecting what they believe. They could have said something else and chose not to.

Now look at the situation instead.

Suppose she had voiced *strong* support. Allied autocrats in the region — the ones the United States needs for basing rights, oil, and intelligence — would immediately accuse Washington of fomenting unrest, with obvious consequences for a dozen bilateral relationships.

Suppose she had voiced *none*, or something dismissive. She would be seen as siding with a brutal theocracy against people risking their lives. Her own party would revolt. She would be condemned by the activists who staff and fund campaigns, would likely lose the next primary, and would drag every politician who had backed her into the same hole.

Between those two walls there is a narrow corridor, and the statement she made is in it. **She had, in any meaningful sense, no choice.** The words were a product of the position, not of the person. Iran is one example; the pattern is everywhere, once you look for it.

## Five Reasons We Refuse to See It

If the situational reading is this available, why does nobody use it? Because five separate forces push the other way, and they reinforce each other.

### 1. The alternative means we are powerless

The only lever the system hands us is the choice of a person. If what an officeholder does is determined mainly by the structure around the office, then replacing the officeholder changes very little — and our one lever is largely decorative.

That is an unbearable conclusion, so we do not reach it. We would rather believe that electing someone else would produce something else. Personal attribution is what optimism requires.

### 2. Liberal culture trains us on free will

We are raised on choice, agency, and individual responsibility. And in personal life the training is not wrong: you really could quit your job tomorrow. You do not, because it would be uncomfortable — not because it is impossible.

Having learned that we are the authors of our own lives, we project the same latitude onto people whose situations are far more constrained than ours, not less.

### 3. We never see the system, only its output

Watching a politician on television, you see thirty seconds of finished product. You do not see the meetings, the polling, the lawyers, the whip counts, the calls from donors, or the campaign in which that same politician asked those same donors for money.

All of the constraint is upstream and invisible. All you get is a person saying words, so a person saying words is what you explain.

### 4. The press knows and does not tell you

Any competent political journalist understands the pressures a hierarchy exerts on the people inside it. Reporting it as *the* story is a different matter.

The first cost is access: the reporter who explains that politicians are structurally unfree gets fewer interviews, fewer briefings, less airtime. The second cost is harder to dismiss. Suppose you tell the public, accurately, that the system rather than the personnel determines the outcomes. What follows? With no alternative on the table, the honest answer is despair, or unrest. So the journalist rationalises, and settles into the posture of the moderate reformer — this particular thing is broken, let us fix this particular thing — and leaves the structure unexamined.

Individuals do the same once they grasp the situation. They tell themselves that the institutions are wiser than they look, that there is accumulated wisdom in old arrangements, that slow reform over centuries reflects some deeper prudence.

Notice that this reasoning depends entirely on there being no alternative. Put a genuine one on the table — direct democracy, deciding issues rather than selecting personnel — and the rationalisation loses its foundation. Suddenly there is somewhere for the analysis to go.

### 5. Rationalisation, and our blindness to it

The last reason is the deepest, because it is about us rather than about them.

Rationalisation is not a defect; it is load-bearing. It lets us live with things we have done and beliefs we half-know are false without either going mad or condemning ourselves continuously.

Consider someone working for an arms manufacturer that sells to dictatorships. He will rationalise staying. The real reason is the salary, but that is too bleak to hold consciously, so he goes looking for a hook — and almost anything will serve. The chief executive appears on television announcing a donation to a children's hospital, and the ledger is balanced. Notice that no actual weighing occurs: nobody sets arms sales to a dictatorship against fifty hospital beds and finds them equal. The point was never to weigh. The point was to resolve a conflict, and any gesture of decency will do it.

We are mostly unaware of this while it happens. Catching yourself mid-rationalisation takes unusual maturity and unusual leisure, and most people have jobs.

**And because we cannot see it in ourselves, we do not see it in others.** Recognising rationalisation everywhere would force us to recognise our own, so we suppress the recognition to avoid the conflict.

Which means we do not see it in politicians either. But politicians rationalise constantly — more than most people, because they face the conflict more often. They will not say so while in office. The memoirs say so at length: the compromises, and the stories told at the time to explain why voting against one's conscience was really the responsible thing to do.

Here is the selection effect that follows. A politician *without* the temperament for this cannot survive the daily gap between what he believes and what he must do. He leaves, or he never climbs. **The ones who reach high office are, by selection, accomplished rationalisers.**

## What This Means

If politicians rationalise, then they are not straightforwardly lying to you on television. That is the uncomfortable conclusion. They may know, somewhere, that they are not doing the best thing — and still sincerely believe they are doing the right thing, because they have talked themselves there. Better to keep supporting this dictator, since a worse one might follow. The role played by the arms manufacturer in shaping that judgement does not come up.

So rationalisation runs on both sides of the screen, and the two halves lock together. The politician rationalises decisions that were effectively made for him by the structure he sits in. The public rationalises alongside him, unable to see the structure — because seeing it in him would mean seeing it in themselves.

That interlock is what keeps the system unquestioned. Not propaganda, and not stupidity. A well-documented cognitive error, pointed at exactly the place where it does the most damage.
