Anyone who wants to make things better eventually faces a choice about scale. You can work locally: your street, your school board, your town council, the people you can actually see. Or you can work globally: national politics, the law, the system itself, the demonstration in the capital. People argue about which is the better use of a life, and the argument has been going for as long as I have been listening to it.

I want to make two points about that argument. The first is about the fact that it exists. The second is my answer.

## The Argument Itself Is the Problem

Reality rarely splits fifty-fifty. When two approaches to the same goal are both widely pursued and nobody can say which is more effective, the likeliest explanation is not that they are equally good. It is that one of them is a great deal better and we have not measured.

That should worry anyone who cares about the goal, because it means an enormous quantity of effort is being misallocated. Half the people who want a better society, roughly, are working at the wrong scale, and we do not know which half. **The persistence of the debate is itself evidence of a gross waste of the scarcest resource reformers have, which is their own time.**

So before giving my answer I want to insist that the question deserves an answer, rather than the usual shrug about everyone contributing in their own way. Everyone contributing in their own way is what you say when you have decided not to find out.

## My Answer: Global

I think the effort belongs at the level of the whole society, and here are the reasons.

**That is where the historical wins came from.** Representative government was not a local achievement that spread. It was fought for at the level of the nation, and in several countries it took a civil war to establish. The abolition of slavery, the franchise for women, the eight-hour day, the end of legal segregation: none of these was a neighbourhood project that scaled up. Each was a change to the rules of the whole game, imposed on every neighbourhood at once, including the ones that did not want it. Local efforts preceded them and prepared them, but the thing that changed lives was the change at the top.

**Local problems are mostly global problems observed locally.** The school in your town is bad because of how schools are funded, staffed and regulated nationally. Housing in your city is unaffordable because of interest rates, tax treatment of land and planning law set far above the city. You can fight the local symptom for a decade and win, and the cause will produce the same symptom in the next town, and eventually in yours again. The local activist is bailing a boat with a hole in it.

**The lever is longer.** A rule changed at the top changes every instance below it. A rule changed at the bottom changes one instance, and a thousand others remain. Where the same effort can move one town or every town, the arithmetic is not close. I have argued that [freedom is roughly conserved and the large failures are organised at the highest level](@/blog/freedom_is_conserved.en.md), and that is another way of stating the same asymmetry: the damage is concentrated up there, so the repair has to be too.

**The opposition is already global.** The interests that shape society, industries, ministries, parties, the large organisations, do not operate at the neighbourhood level. They lobby the legislature and fund the campaigns. A reformer who confines himself to local work has chosen a battlefield the adversary does not bother to contest, and that is not because the adversary is careless.

## The Case for Local, and Why It Does Not Persuade Me

The honest counter-argument is that local work is where you can actually see results, where feedback is fast, where you cannot fool yourself, and where you are dealing with people rather than abstractions. National politics is a place where good intentions go to be captured, and where a lifetime of effort can produce nothing you can point to.

All true, and I feel the force of it. But notice that these are arguments about the reformer's satisfaction, not about the outcome. Fast feedback is pleasant. Visible results are pleasant. Neither is evidence that the world changed more.

There is also a quieter argument: that local work is training, that people who learn to organise a town later organise a country. Sometimes. But a great many people spend their whole lives on the training and never sit the exam, and the movement that was supposed to scale up stays exactly where it was.

## Why We Default to Local Anyway

If the case for global is this strong, why does so much effort go local?

Because global effort is frightening in a way local effort is not. The system at the top is designed to be hard to move, and it is hard to move, and a person who spends five years trying and fails has nothing to show. Local work offers the certainty of having done something. That certainty is real, and it is worth something to the person who has it. It is not worth much to anyone else.

I have written about a related question, [which level of government you would give up first](@/blog/give_up_the_city_before_the_nation.en.md), and the answer there was the same: the national level is where the harm comes from, so it is where the attention belongs. This post is the same claim from the reformer's side of the table. **If you want to spend a life making things better, spend it where the rules are made, not where they are suffered.** It is less satisfying. It is also the only scale at which the wins have ever been won.
