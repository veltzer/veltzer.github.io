We have telescopes that see the edge of the observable universe, machines that
read the genome, and a library of all human knowledge in every pocket. And yet
most of humanity organises its life around things that aren't true. That isn't
an accident or a phase. The human mind wasn't built to track reality, and
almost every institution we've created makes it worse.

## Religion: the original untethering

Religion is the oldest and biggest reason. Billions of people believe in gods,
souls, sin, heaven, hell, and divine plans, without a shred of evidence for
any of them. These aren't harmless private beliefs. They decide what people
eat, whom they may marry, how they treat women, what their children learn, and
even whether they may end their own lives.

Worse, religion trains the habit of mind that makes every other delusion easy:
that faith, believing without evidence, is a virtue. A mind taught from
childhood that the most important truths are beyond question has been prepared
to accept anything said with enough authority.

## Our brains were built for survival, not truth

Evolution didn't select for accuracy. It selected for staying alive and
fitting into the group. So we see patterns where there are none, assume
intention behind random events, remember what confirms our beliefs and forget
what contradicts them, and feel certain long before we have reason to be. A
rustle in the grass was safer read as a predator than as wind. Those same
instincts now produce astrology, conspiracy theories, and lucky socks.

## Tribe beats truth

For most of history, being expelled from the group meant death. Being wrong
about the world usually didn't. So people learned to believe what their group
believes. Today that instinct drives politics, nationalism, and identity.
People don't reason their way to their views; they inherit them from their
side and then defend them. Facts that threaten the tribe get dismissed,
however solid they are.

## Ideologies: secular religions

Leaving God behind doesn't automatically bring people back to reality. The
twentieth century replaced religion with ideologies that promised a perfect
future: communism, fascism, utopian nationalism. They had their own
scriptures, prophets, heretics, and paradises. They were wrong about human
nature in the same way the churches were, and they killed tens of millions of
people proving it.

## The modern amplifiers

Technology should have fixed this. Instead it supercharged it. Social media
rewards outrage and certainty, not accuracy. Algorithms feed each person more
of what they already believe. Anyone can find a community that confirms any
fantasy, from a flat Earth to miracle cures. Misinformation travels faster
than corrections, and now AI can produce convincing fakes at almost no cost.

## History: stories we mistake for knowledge

The problem with history isn't only that the winners write it. It goes much
deeper. Take the Vietnam War. It was filmed by thousands of cameras, covered
by millions of articles and editorials, and recorded in government archives,
and many of its witnesses are still alive. Yet fifty years later we still
can't agree on why it happened, whether it could have been won, or what it
meant. The more evidence there is, the more interpretations pile up.

Now think about the wars between Rome and Carthage. We have a handful of
documents, almost all written long after the events by people with their own
agendas, and not a single living witness. If we can't settle the truth about
Vietnam, what chance do we have with the Punic Wars? Very little. What we call
ancient history is mostly educated storytelling: a plausible narrative
stretched over a few scraps of evidence, then taught as fact.

The whole enterprise of history, presented as knowledge of the past, is itself
untethered from reality. At best it gives us useful stories. It rarely gives
us truth.

## Social sciences: theories too grand to test

The social sciences dress up as science, but most of them aren't. Their
theories are far too grandiose ever to be tested. Freud explained the whole
human mind with the Oedipus complex and the unconscious. Grand sociological
theories explain all of society through power, structure, or culture. No
experiment could ever prove them wrong, and a theory that can't be proven
wrong isn't knowledge. It's a story with footnotes.

In psychology, the only part that deserves to be taken seriously is
experimental psychology, and only when its results replicate. Everything else
is simply junk. Even the experimental part has a problem: during the
replication crisis, a large share of famous findings failed when other
researchers repeated them. If the most rigorous corner of the field struggles
to stand up, the grand theories built on top of it have nothing to stand on at
all.

## Philosophy: words about words

Almost all of philosophy belongs on this list. For two and a half thousand
years, philosophers have argued about being, mind, free will, and the meaning
of life, and almost nothing has ever been settled. Grand metaphysical systems
like Hegel's or Heidegger's are built from words that refer to other words,
never to anything that could be observed or measured. Their obscurity is often
mistaken for depth. A field that never resolves its central questions isn't
discovering truth. It's producing commentary.

The exception is the philosophy of science, which asks how we can actually
know things, what counts as evidence, and when a theory deserves to be
believed. That part is tethered to reality, because its whole subject is the
method that keeps us tethered. Much of the rest is clever talk.

## Literature: no obligation to reality

Literature has no constraint at all to track how people actually behave. A
novelist can make characters think, feel, and act however the story needs, and
nobody checks it against reality. No data, no experiments, no replication. The
only test is whether readers find it moving or convincing, and that isn't the
same thing as true.

Yet people treat great novels as deep insight into human nature. They learn
what love, war, courage, and betrayal are "really" like from authors who
simply made it up. Neat motives, meaningful endings, and characters who change
at exactly the right moment feel real because they're well written, not
because people actually behave that way. A culture that takes its
understanding of human beings from fiction has drifted far from reality.

Worse, there's an incentive mechanism pushing authors away from reality. Real
human behaviour is murky, complicated, and often unflattering. Readers don't
want that. They want stories that stroke their egos: the reader identifies
with the hero, the good are rewarded, and people like them turn out to be
deep, decent, and right. Authors who give readers what they want sell more
books. So the market rewards authors for flattering their readers, which means
lying to them, and every flattering lie pulls the reader a little further from
reality.

## Cinema: fiction that looks like evidence

Cinema runs the same incentive at a much larger scale. A film can cost
hundreds of millions of dollars and must please tens of millions of people to
earn it back, so the pressure to flatter the audience is enormous. Heroes win,
villains are obvious, love conquers all, and problems resolve in two hours.

What makes cinema more dangerous than books is that it looks real. Faces,
voices, places, explosions, all rendered so convincingly that the brain files
them almost as memories. Many people's picture of history, war, crime,
courtrooms, hospitals, and love comes mostly from films, and they don't notice
that what they "know" was invented by screenwriters and tuned by test
audiences.

## Advertising: flattering lies as an industry

If literature and cinema flatter by accident of incentive, advertising does it
on purpose. Hundreds of billions of dollars a year are spent persuading people
that a car will make them free, a perfume will make them desired, and a phone
will make them creative. Nobody believes the claims literally, yet they work,
because they aren't aimed at reason. An entire industry exists to attach
feelings to products that have nothing to do with them.

## Economics and the stock market: confident forecasts, no accountability

Economics wears the clothes of science, with equations and models, but its
forecasts fail again and again. Almost no major institution predicted the 2008
crisis. Yet economists keep forecasting with the same confidence, and nobody
pays a price for being wrong.

The stock market takes this further. Prices are supposed to reflect the real
value of companies, but they move on mood, rumour, herd behaviour, and
stories. Bubbles inflate around tulips, railways, dot-coms, and meme stocks,
then burst. An army of analysts and commentators explains every move after it
happens, as confidently as if they had predicted it, and most professional
fund managers fail to beat a simple index over time. It's a huge machine for
turning narratives into prices.

## Nutrition and wellness: fads dressed as science

Few fields reverse themselves as often. Fat was the enemy, then sugar, then
carbohydrates. Eggs were deadly, then fine. Much of nutrition science rests on
weak observational studies, and the wellness industry builds on top of that
with supplements, detoxes, and miracle diets that are barely tested at all.
People make daily decisions about their bodies based on headlines that will be
contradicted next year.

## Politics: winning without being right

Elections reward whoever tells the most appealing story, not whoever describes
the world accurately. Slogans beat analysis, and promises are rarely checked
against results. A politician who tells voters the murky truth about
trade-offs loses to one who tells them they're right and someone else is to
blame. Democracy, for all its virtues, has no built-in mechanism that rewards
accuracy.

## Comfort over truth

Under all of this sits a simple fact: reality is often unpleasant. We die.
There's no cosmic justice. The universe doesn't care about us. Many people
prefer a comforting story to a hard truth, and an entire economy, religious,
political, and commercial, exists to sell them those stories.

## The exceptions: where reality pushes back

So what's left? Mathematics, the hard sciences, engineering, and medicine when
it's tested in proper clinical trials. These fields aren't tethered because
their practitioners are wiser or more honest. They're tethered because reality
pushes back. A proof either holds or fails. A prediction either matches the
measurement or it doesn't. A bridge stands or falls. A drug beats the placebo
or flunks its trial.

That's the whole lesson of this list. Humans drift from reality whenever
nothing stops them, and they stay tethered only where a hard, impartial test
forces them to. Every field without such a test, however prestigious, ends up
producing stories.

## Getting back to earth

The way back isn't complicated, just hard. Demand evidence. Change your mind
when the evidence changes. Treat certainty with suspicion, especially your
own. Teach children how to think, not what to believe. Science, imperfect as
it is, remains the only method humanity has found that consistently corrects
its own mistakes. The rest is wishful thinking.

## The other side

Not everyone would accept this picture. Defenders of religion argue that faith
gives people meaning, community, and moral structure that secular society
struggles to replace, and that many great scientists were believers. Some
psychologists argue that a few positive illusions, like mild optimism,
actually help people function. Others point out that humanity has never been
better informed: literacy, life expectancy, and scientific knowledge are at
historic highs, which suggests we're more tethered to reality than ever, just
not evenly.
