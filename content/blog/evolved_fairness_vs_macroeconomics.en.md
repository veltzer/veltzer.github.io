*Why our deepest moral intuitions about fairness, sharing, and protection often backfire when scaled to complex global economies.*

For roughly 99% of human evolutionary history, our ancestors lived in small, nomadic foraging bands of 30 to 150 individuals. Survival in those intimate groups depended on face-to-face reciprocity, strict egalitarian sharing of windfalls, visceral disgust toward selfish profiteering, and swift punishment of free riders.

Today, we carry that exact same Paleolithic moral hardware into a globalized economy of eight billion strangers coordinated by abstract price signals. The result is a persistent, tragic friction: policies that feel deeply righteous to our evolved moral intuitions routinely violate fundamental laws of supply, demand, and incentives, harming the very people those policies are meant to protect.

## The Two Worlds Hypothesis: Folk Economics vs. The Extended Order

Nobel laureate Friedrich Hayek, in his 1988 book [The Fatal Conceit: The Errors of Socialism](https://www.abebooks.com/9780226320663/Fatal-Conceit-Errors-Socialism-Volume-0226320669/plp) (see also the [Cato Institute analysis of Hayek's Extended Order](https://www.cato.org/regulation/spring-2018/fatal-conceit)), observed that modern humans inhabit "two worlds" simultaneously. The first is the micro-cosmos of the family, friendship circle, and small tribe, where visible solidarity and deliberate sharing work well. The second is the **extended order** of the market, where millions of strangers cooperate without knowing one another by responding to impersonal price signals.

Cognitive scientists Pascal Boyer and Michael Bang Petersen formalize this evolutionary mismatch in their landmark study, [Folk-Economic Beliefs: An Evolutionary Cognitive Model](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/folkeconomic-beliefs-an-evolutionary-cognitive-model/7D84E452710ACCC6A35D49B8590B614F) (*Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, Vol. 41). They demonstrate that laypeople rely on specialized, automatic mental inference systems that evolved for ancestral small-scale sociality, such as free-rider detection, fairness-based partner choice, and coalitional psychology. When exposed to modern mass markets, these mental modules generate predictable misconceptions:

- **Emporiophobia (fear of markets):** Viewing impersonal market exchange and the profit motive as inherently predatory rather than cooperative.
- **The labor theory of value:** Assuming value comes strictly from visible physical effort rather than risk, capital, or consumer subjective valuation.
- **Faith in price controls:** Believing that statutory price ceilings or floors directly achieve their intended outcomes without altering supply.

Economist Paul H. Rubin, in [Zero-Sum Thinking and Economic Policy](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/zerosum-thinking-and-economic-policy/C38927254280E3F6648F58E36C7D73B4) and his foundational research on [Folk Economics](https://econ.biu.ac.il/sites/econ/files/shared/people/LevyDaniel/levy-and-snir-oxoe-2022.pdf), points to the root cause: in the ancestral environment, technological growth was virtually nonexistent and resources were fixed. If one band member took a larger piece of meat, another went hungry. Consequently, evolved "folk economics" is obsessed with static wealth allocation rather than **dynamic wealth production**. Naive intuition treats prices as mere tools for dividing an existing pie rather than signals that incentivize people to bake a larger one.

Behavioral economists Ernst Fehr and Klaus M. Schmidt proved mathematically how this divide operates in [A Theory of Fairness, Competition, and Cooperation](https://www.research-collection.ethz.ch/server/api/core/bitstreams/844bbb1d-1df0-496c-b7e2-b3d7d43e6fe4/content) (*Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 1999). In two-person bargaining experiments like the Ultimatum Game, humans exhibit strong **inequity aversion**, rejecting unequal splits even at a personal cost. Yet Fehr and Schmidt show that as soon as you move from bilateral bargaining to a competitive market with multiple buyers or sellers, individual fairness preferences can no longer dictate the outcome: supply and demand take over.

When voters and policymakers force tribal fairness norms onto macroeconomic systems, four classic policy paradoxes emerge.

## 1. Organ Procurement: When Moral Repugnance Costs Lives

Nowhere is the clash between evolved emotion and economic reality more lethal than in the shortage of human organs for transplant. When someone proposes compensating organ donors financially, most people experience an immediate visceral recoil: a sentiment that putting a price tag on part of the human body is degrading or exploitative.

Nobel laureate Alvin E. Roth, in [Repugnance as a Constraint on Markets](https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/jep.21.3.37) (*Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 2007), coined the term repugnant markets to describe transactions that willing participants want to make, but third parties ban out of moral disgust. In the United States, the National Organ Transplant Act (NOTA) criminalizes offering financial compensation to organ donors, enforcing an altruistic price ceiling of $0 ([NBER Working Paper 34140 on Organ Procurement Reform](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w34140/w34140.pdf)).

In macroeconomic terms, a binding price ceiling of zero guarantees a chronic shortage:

- **The equilibrium price of eliminating the shortage:** Nobel laureate Gary S. Becker and Julio Jorge Elías, in [Introducing Incentives in the Market for Live and Cadaveric Organ Donations](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.21.3.3) (*Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 2007; see also the [EconStor kidney market analysis](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/272320/1/1800473052.pdf)), calculated that a monetary incentive of roughly **$15,000 per kidney** would expand organ supply enough to completely eliminate transplant waiting lists and prevent thousands of deaths each year, while raising the total cost of transplant surgery by no more than **12%**.
- **The hidden penalty on altruism:** Research by Philip J. Held, Frank McCormick, and colleagues in [Removing Disincentives to Kidney Donation: A Quantitative Analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6683718/) reveals that living kidney donors actually face nearly **$38,000 in uncompensated disincentives**, including lost wages, travel costs, discomfort, and forfeiting a spare kidney they might later need for a family member. Because of this implicit penalty, only **5,811 patients (4.6%)** of the 125,400 Americans diagnosed with end-stage renal disease in 2017 received a kidney from a living donor.
- **The fiscal and human dividend:** Held et al. demonstrate that simply reimbursing those $38,000 in donor disincentives would generate **12,500 additional kidney donations per year**, halving the adult waiting list within four years, saving taxpayers **$1.3 billion annually** ($146,000 saved in dialysis costs per transplant), and creating **$14 billion per year** in net societal welfare. Because direct payment remains politically taboo, economists like Roth have had to design elaborate non-price workarounds like paired [Kidney Exchange](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/jecper/v21y2007i3p37-58.html) chains just to save lives within the boundaries of human moral repugnance.

## 2. Income Disparity: Ancestral Cheater-Detection vs. Superstar Scale

In a foraging band, extreme wealth disparity was almost always the result of physical dominance, hoarding, or cheating the group's sharing norms. Yet psychologists Christina Starmans, Mark Sheskin, and Paul Bloom show in [Why People Prefer Unequal Societies](https://equitablegrowth.org/must-read-christina-starmans-mark-sheskin-and-paul-bloom-why-people-prefer-unequal-societies/) (*Nature Human Behaviour*, 2017; see also the [University of Toronto StarLab overview](https://starlab.utoronto.ca/unequalsocieties.html)) that humans do not actually crave strict equality of outcome. Rather, children and adults across cultures naturally prefer fair inequality over unfair equality, rewarding harder workers and higher contributors proportionally.

Why, then, does modern macroeconomic income dispersion trigger such intense moral outrage? Because our ancestral intuition expects incomes to scale linearly with hours worked or sweat expended, and assumes that if billionaires exist, their wealth must have been subtracted from everyone else ([Paul Rubin on folk beliefs about inequality](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/zerosum-thinking-and-economic-policy/C38927254280E3F6648F58E36C7D73B4)).

In a modern macroeconomy, however, income disparity is amplified by non-linear scale effects that did not exist in the Pleistocene. Economist Sherwin Rosen explained this mechanism in his classic 1981 *American Economic Review* paper, [The Economics of Superstars](https://scispace.com/papers/the-economics-of-superstars-3h9gizahm6) (see also the [Chicago Booth Review on Superstar Firms](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/market-power-superstar-companies-growing) and [the Tutor2u breakdown of the Economics of Superstars](https://www.tutor2u.net/economics/blog/the-economics-of-superstars)):

- **Imperfect substitution:** In high-skill fields, quality is not additive. Hearing two mediocre singers is not the equivalent of hearing one world-class vocalist; hiring two average software architects does not replace one transformative systems designer.
- **Joint consumption and zero marginal replication cost:** In 1880, the best singer in the world could only perform for a thousand people in a concert hall. Today, digital software, global supply chains, and mass media allow a single creator, firm, or innovator to serve billions of consumers simultaneously at near-zero marginal cost of replication. For example, while the quality gap between soccer players in the English Premier League and the Scottish Premiership is modest, global broadcasting allows the English league to command over **£1 billion per year** in television rights compared to less than **£20 million** in Scotland.

When policymakers treat positive-sum wealth creation as zero-sum theft and impose punitive marginal tax rates or rigid wage compression, they dull the exact price signals that allocate talent and capital toward high-value innovation.

## 3. Minimum Wage: Compassionate Floors vs. Marginal Productivity

Few policies appeal more directly to evolved fairness than raising the statutory minimum wage. Our moral intuition frames the labor market as a bilateral tug-of-war between a wealthy employer and a vulnerable worker: forcing the employer to pay more feels like a pure, costless victory for fairness ([Rubin on folk beliefs about labor regulation](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/zerosum-thinking-and-economic-policy/C38927254280E3F6648F58E36C7D73B4)).

Macroeconomic law, however, dictates that labor demand is downward-sloping. Firms hire workers only so long as the worker's marginal revenue product exceeds the total cost of employing them. When a statutory wage floor is set above the market-clearing productivity level of entry-level or low-skill workers, employers adjust on multiple margins: automating tasks, cutting hours, reducing non-wage benefits, raising consumer prices, or eliminating entry-level roles altogether.

- **Disemployment among the least skilled:** In an extensive review of more than 100 minimum-wage studies since the 1990s, economists David Neumark and William Wascher ([San Francisco Fed Economic Letter](https://www.frbsf.org/research-and-insights/publications/economic-letter/2015/12/effects-of-minimum-wage-on-employment/) and [NBER Working Paper 4570](https://ideas.repec.org/p/nbr/nberwo/4570.html)) found that nearly **two-thirds of studies** identify negative employment effects for the lowest-skilled workers, with employment elasticities for teenagers and young adults typically ranging between **-0.1 and -0.2** (a 10% wage hike reduces youth employment by 1% to 2%), alongside reductions in on-the-job training and school enrollment ([NBER Working Paper 6536](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w6536/w6536.pdf)).
- **The national trade-off quantified:** The nonpartisan Congressional Budget Office, in [The Effects on Employment and Family Income of Increasing the Federal Minimum Wage](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/55410) (and its [2021 Raise the Wage Act analysis summarized by PGPF](https://www.pgpf.org/article/heres-how-raising-the-minimum-wage-would-affect-everything-from-household-incomes-to-the-national-debt/)), illustrates that minimum wage hikes are not a free lunch. Phasing in a $15 federal minimum wage was projected to raise wages for roughly **17 million workers** directly and lift **0.9 million to 1.3 million people** out of poverty, but at a median cost of **1.3 million lost jobs**, higher consumer prices, and lower aggregate real output.

The workers priced out of the market altogether are disproportionately the youngest, least experienced individuals who most need that first rung on the career ladder.

## 4. Environmental Housing Regulations: Local Stewardship vs. Carbon Sprawl

Our evolved psychology is fiercely territorial and suspicious of outsiders who alter the local landscape for profit. In affluent, high-productivity cities, incumbent residents frequently unite around environmental preservation, neighborhood character, and anti-developer fairness norms to restrict high-density apartment construction.

When evaluated through macroeconomic and environmental data, these well-intentioned local regulations produce catastrophic national side effects:

- **The "Green Paradox" of coastal land-use restrictions:** Economists Edward L. Glaeser and Matthew E. Kahn, in [The Greenness of Cities: Carbon Dioxide Emissions and Urban Development](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/juecon/v67y2010i3p404-418.html) (*Journal of Urban Economics*, 2010) and Glaeser's Brookings analysis [Reforming Land Use Regulations](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/reforming-land-use-regulations/), measured household carbon footprints across U.S. metropolitan areas. They found that per-household carbon emissions are substantially lower in dense, mild-climate coastal California than in the rest of the country. When local environmental laws block infill housing in coastal California, those families do not vanish: they move to sprawling, car-dependent, air-conditioning-intensive sunbelt metros like Houston and Las Vegas. Preventing local construction in the name of environmentalism actually **increases global carbon emissions**.
- **The regressive "Zoning Tax":** In [The Economic Implications of Housing Supply](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.32.1.3) (*Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 2018) and [Restricting Residential Construction](https://www.lincolninst.edu/publications/conference-papers/restricting-residential-construction/) (Lincoln Institute of Land Policy), Edward Glaeser and Joseph Gyourko show that land-use restrictions drive a massive wedge (an implicit "zoning tax") between physical construction costs and home prices, transferring wealth from young renters to older property owners and costing the San Francisco Bay Area alone roughly **$2 billion per year** while shrinking its population by **200,000 residents**. Similarly, structural research by Jaehee Song in [Unpacking Housing Supply Constraints: Zoning Regulations and Construction Costs](https://jaeheesong.com/assets/papers/zoning_mapc.pdf) (2026) demonstrates that relaxing multiple constraints together (cutting regulatory project costs by $50,000 and removing minimum lot-size mandates) boosts new housing supply by **130% to 140%**.
- **Spatial misallocation and macroeconomic drag:** Economists Chang-Tai Hsieh and Enrico Moretti, in [Housing Constraints and Spatial Misallocation](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/mac.20170388) (*American Economic Journal: Macroeconomics*, 2019), showed that artificial housing caps in high-productivity hubs (New York, San Francisco, and San Jose) lock workers out of the cities where their labor is most valuable, originally estimating a **3.7% loss to aggregate U.S. GDP** (with subsequent methodological refinements by Brian Greaney in his 2026 [Comment on Housing Constraints and Spatial Misallocation](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/mac.20230141) confirming that relaxing urban land-use barriers yields positive net national output gains).

## Bridging the Gap: Designing Policy for Both Worlds

Recognizing the conflict between evolutionary psychology and macroeconomic law does not mean abandoning compassion or fairness. Instead, it means separating our **moral ends** (saving patients in kidney failure, lifting low-income families, curbing carbon emissions, and expanding opportunity) from Stone Age policy means (price controls, market bans, and local supply blockades).

When we work with price signals and incentives rather than against them (by reimbursing organ donors' costs, expanding Earned Income Tax Credits or human-capital training instead of pricing entry-level workers out of jobs, and legalizing dense, transit-oriented housing in productive green cities) we honor our evolved desire for a fairer society while harnessing the macroeconomic laws that make shared prosperity possible.
