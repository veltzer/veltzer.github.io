A society needs its citizens to trust their government. This is not a sentiment; it is an operating requirement. The pandemic made it visible: a public health measure works only if enough people believe the body issuing it is acting in good faith, and where that belief was absent, no quantity of evidence substituted for it. The same holds for paying taxes, obeying laws that are not enforced at every corner, accepting a court's verdict, showing up when the country is attacked. A government that is not trusted must coerce, and a government that must coerce is expensive, brittle, and usually short-lived.

So trust is not a nicety. It is load-bearing. And I want to argue that representative democracy, by its own design and not by accident, works against it.

## The Adversarial Half

Consider what an election is, structurally. Two sides contend, one wins, the winner governs everyone. That is the design, and it has a consequence nobody built into the civics lesson.

On the day after the election, roughly half the country has just watched the side it regards as its adversary take control of the state. Not a coalition it dislikes. An *enemy*, because the campaign has spent months teaching everyone to see the other side that way, since that is what wins campaigns. That half now believes, with the full backing of everything it has been told, that an alien and malign power is running the country.

**Distrust of the government is therefore not a malfunction of the system. It is the system's output, for about half the population, every single cycle.** And the halves alternate, so over time nearly everyone has spent years believing the state was in the hands of people who wished them ill.

Notice how peculiar this is. We have built a mechanism for choosing a government whose side effect is to certify, to a large fraction of the governed, that the government is not theirs. Then we wonder why they will not take the vaccine.

## The Consensual Half

The obvious repair is to bring the two sides closer together. If the parties disagree only on details, if they are seen as two teams of broadly reasonable people quibbling over the margins, then the losing half need not feel occupied. The government is theirs too, more or less. Many countries have drifted this way, and it does reduce the sense of alien rule.

But watch what it does to the other requirement.

If the two options on the ballot are nearly the same, then the election decides almost nothing. The majority's vote no longer changes what happens. And the idea that *the people are at the helm*, the idea that was supposed to make the government legitimate in the first place, loses its content. Why should I participate, or care, or feel that the state is mine, if my choice between the two was a choice between two shades of the same policy?

So trust in the government's good faith rises, and trust in the claim that the people rule falls. You have traded one kind of legitimacy for the other.

## The Paradox

Put the two halves side by side.

**The further apart the alternatives are, the more the losing half distrusts the government. The closer together they are, the weaker the idea that the people govern at all.** There is no setting of the dial at which both requirements are met. Move toward polarisation and you manufacture enemies of the state; move toward consensus and you manufacture the suspicion, entirely correct, that the vote was theatre.

This is not a problem of the current parties, or the current media, or the current level of rancour. It is a property of the design: choose a government by contest between sides, and you must either make the sides different enough for the contest to matter, which alienates the losers, or similar enough not to alienate them, which empties the contest.

I have written about [why the system asks what wins rather than what is right](@/blog/politics_asks_what_wins.en.md); this is the mirror image, seen from the citizen's side. The citizen is asked to trust a machine whose every setting undermines one of the two reasons to trust it.

## What Escapes the Paradox

The paradox lives entirely inside the choice-between-sides structure. Remove that structure and it dissolves.

If the public decided questions rather than choosing rulers, there would be no losing half in the relevant sense. You lose particular votes, as you lose arguments, and you win others; you are never handed over to an adversary for four years. Nobody governs *you*, so there is nobody to distrust in that way. And at the same time the outcome plainly follows from what the majority wanted on that question, so the claim that the people decide is not a slogan but a description of what just happened.

Both requirements met at once, by the same mechanism, because the thing that set them against each other was the contest for control. Take away the contest and trust stops being a paradox and becomes what it should have been all along: the ordinary attitude of people toward decisions they took part in.
