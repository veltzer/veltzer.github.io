The debate about a universal basic income is conducted almost entirely in the language of economics. Can we afford it, will people stop working, what happens to inflation. Those are real questions and I do not dismiss them. But I think they are not what the fight is about, and the tell is who is on which side.

## What Fear of Losing a Job Does

Most people, most of the time, are afraid of losing their job. Not in a panic, but as a steady background condition that shapes what they say and do not say. The mortgage depends on the job, the children's school depends on it, the health insurance in many countries depends on it. A person in that position calculates before speaking, and the calculation usually comes out the same way: keep quiet.

So they keep quiet about the manager who is incompetent, about the product that does not work, about the accounting that is not quite right, about the colleague who is being mistreated. Not because they are cowards. Because the cost of speaking is concentrated on them and the benefit is spread over everybody.

**A basic income changes the arithmetic of that calculation.** Not by making anyone rich, but by making the loss of a job survivable. A person who knows he will eat next month whatever his employer does can afford to say what he thinks, and a workforce of such people is a different thing from a workforce of people who cannot.

## The Precedent

We have seen this before, and the case is instructive.

Sexual harassment at work was, for most of the twentieth century, pervasive and almost never reported. It did not stop being pervasive because men became better. It became reportable when the women subjected to it could afford to report it: when a woman who lost her job over a complaint would not be destitute, because standards of living had risen, because unemployment insurance existed, because a second household income was no longer a luxury. Once the cost of speaking fell below the cost of silence, people spoke, and the thing that had been normal for a century became a scandal in a decade.

Nothing about the underlying behaviour had changed. What changed was the price of objecting to it. **Rights that exist on paper are exercised only by people who can afford the consequences**, and the consequences are mostly economic.

## Who Would Lose

Now ask who benefits from a population that keeps quiet.

Employers, obviously, in the narrow sense that a docile workforce is cheaper to manage and harder to organise. But the benefit runs wider than any single firm. The whole arrangement by which a few thousand people in politics and business make the decisions and the rest accept them rests on the rest having too much to lose. A public that could afford to strike, to refuse, to testify, to walk out, to spend a year on a campaign instead of a job, would be a public with power it currently lacks.

Corporations understand this. So do the media that depend on corporate advertising, and the politicians whose campaigns corporations fund. **A basic income is, among other things, a transfer of leverage from the people who employ to the people who are employed**, and the people who employ can count.

That is why the opposition to it is so much fiercer than the economics warrants. Plenty of transfers of comparable size pass without much fuss when they flow upward: agricultural subsidies, bank rescues, tax treatment of capital. A transfer that flows downward and, worse, gives its recipients the freedom to say no, meets resistance out of all proportion to its cost.

## What I Am Not Claiming

I am not claiming the economic objections are dishonest. Some are serious. The financing is genuinely hard, and the labour-market effects are genuinely uncertain, and anyone proposing a basic income owes an answer on both.

I am claiming that if the economic questions were all answered tomorrow, the opposition would not go away, because the economic questions are not the source of it. The source is the thing the proposal would actually do, which is to make a large number of people harder to frighten. Everyone who currently benefits from their being frightened knows it, and they argue about inflation because it is more respectable than saying so.

The pattern is familiar from every other freedom people have had to pry loose from the system, which I have written about [before](@/blog/organizational_freedom_cost.en.md): the freedoms that get granted easily are the ones that do not shift power. This one would. Watch how it is argued against and you can measure exactly how much.
