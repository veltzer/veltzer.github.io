Here is an observation that is easy to make and hard to absorb: **institutions almost never cease to exist.**

Not that they never decline, or shrink, or become embarrassing. They persist. Religious institutions, economic institutions, bureaucracies, professional bodies, regulatory agencies, international organisations — go looking for examples of one that simply ended because it had finished its work or been discredited, and the list is remarkably short.

Institutions get absorbed, renamed, restructured, and merged. They almost never stop.

## The Usual Explanation, and Why It Is Not Enough

The standard account is inertia. Organisations have staff, budgets, buildings, and legal existence, and dismantling them takes effort nobody is paid to expend. This is true and it explains some of it.

But inertia does not explain the interesting cases: institutions that persist through **sustained and explicit public hostility.** Bodies that most people would abolish if asked directly, whose purposes have visibly lapsed, which are the subject of routine ridicule — and which are still there a generation later, funded and staffed.

Inertia explains why nobody bothers to close a body nobody thinks about. It does not explain survival against active opposition.

## What the Survival Demonstrates

That is where the observation becomes an argument rather than a curiosity.

If an institution can outlive the public's settled desire to be rid of it, then **the public's desire is not what governs.** That is not a metaphor or a complaint about how things feel. It is a straightforward inference from an outcome: given a sustained popular preference for X, and a persistent outcome of not-X, the popular preference is not the operative variable.

And notice the strength of the test. We are not asking whether the public gets its way on subtle questions requiring expertise, where deference might be appropriate. We are asking about the crudest possible case — *should this thing exist at all* — where the public's view is unambiguous and easy to survey.

If popular will cannot prevail even there, the claim that we live under popular government needs qualifying rather badly.

## The Real Distribution of Power

What the persistence reveals is that institutions hold power out of all proportion to their size, and that the distribution of power in society is fundamentally anti-populist.

An institution of a few thousand people, with no electoral mandate and often with declining public support, routinely outlasts the preferences of millions. It does this without conspiracy. It does it because it is **organised, permanent, and attentive**, and the millions are **unorganised, transient, and distracted.**

This is Olson's asymmetry in its purest form. The institution's staff have their entire livelihood riding on its continuation; every one of them will spend real effort defending it. The citizen who thinks it should be abolished has, at most, a mild preference, and will spend nothing. A few thousand people who care enormously defeat a few million who care slightly — every time, without anyone behaving improperly.

## The Conclusion I Draw

**The longevity is the proof.** An institution that outlives the public's desire to be rid of it has demonstrated, by simply continuing to exist, that public desire is not the mechanism by which such questions are settled.

You do not need to look for hidden power or trace donations to reach this. The evidence is entirely in the open, in the survival statistics of organisations. Count the institutions that have ended against those that have persisted through hostility, and you have measured how much of a democracy you are living in.
