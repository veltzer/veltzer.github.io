Most of the talk about AI and software is about coders: will AI write the code, will programmers lose their jobs. I want to report something else, a plain observation from my own work.

Since AI came along, I am able to do a great deal more. And when I look at what that extra work actually is, most of it falls under one heading: architecture. Not typing code, but deciding how systems should be shaped. **AI has not made the architect obsolete. It has given the profession a rebirth.**

## The old rhythm

Before AI, software development was slow. Even when I worked as an architect, a real architectural decision came along perhaps once every six months, sometimes once a year. Which database, which messaging layer, how to split the system into services. Each choice was followed by months of implementation before anyone knew whether it had been right.

That pace shaped the job. Architecture was a matter of a few big, careful bets. You thought hard, wrote documents, held reviews, and then waited. Over an entire career, an architect might see only a few dozen of their decisions play out.

## The new rhythm

With AI writing much of the code, I can now engineer a whole system in a couple of days. In those days I decide on dozens of architectural issues, and I see the consequences of each one almost immediately.

My loop of understanding has become dramatically shorter. I try an idea, and within hours it either works or it fails. Then I try the next one. **In a matter of days I am learning more about architecture than I learned in the previous several years.**

## Quantity becomes quality

Hegel had a name for this, later made famous by Engels: quantitative change turning into qualitative change. Heat water degree by degree and nothing much happens, until at one particular degree it boils and becomes something else.

Speeding up a process can change the nature of the process. That is what happened to software architecture. It is not the old activity done faster. It is a different activity. The old architect placed a few big, careful bets. The new architect places many small, wild ones, because each costs a day instead of a year.

## Closing the loop

The slow rhythm had a worse flaw than slowness. When you make a decision once a year, you may not be around to see its results. Architects change projects and change companies. By the time a bad choice shows its cost, the person who made it has often moved on.

So the loop never closes. The architect never learns whether the decision was right, and the person who pays for it is someone else, usually the team that inherits the system. That is bad for learning and bad for accountability.

A short loop fixes both. When the consequences arrive in hours, the person who made the call is the person who lives with it, and learns from it.

## Architecture as experiment

The short loop also allows something the old paradigm never did: real experimentation. When a decision costs a year, you cannot afford to try three options. You pick one, defend it in meetings, and hope.

When a decision costs a day, you can build all three and compare them. Architecture stops being an act of prediction and becomes an act of measurement. The architect works less like a planner drawing blueprints and more like a scientist running experiments, keeping what works and discarding what does not.

## A rebirth, not an ending

For years, architecture was the part of software you could only learn slowly, through a handful of decisions spread across a career, many of whose results you never saw. AI has changed that. With the code cheap and fast, the decisions are what remain, and they can now be made, tested and learned from at a pace that was never possible before.

The architect is not being replaced. The architect is finally getting the feedback loop every other craft takes for granted. That is why I call it a rebirth.
