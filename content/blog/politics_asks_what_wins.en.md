I was watching a political panel when a pundit was asked whether any position was too far left. She answered immediately and fluently — by naming the positions that would cost a candidate the election.

She did not address which positions were *correct*. It did not appear to occur to her that this was a different question, or that it was the one she had been asked. The substitution was seamless, and nobody on the panel noticed.

That substitution is the disease, and it is worth being precise about how deep it goes.

## The Question That Is Never Asked

The system trains everyone inside it — pundits, communications staff, candidates themselves — to think exclusively about what can win. This is not corruption. Nobody is taking bribes. It is what the job rewards, applied consistently for a career.

And the result is not that the question "what is right?" loses to the question "what wins?" **The result is that it never gets asked at all.** These people are not weighing correctness against electability and choosing electability. They have no vocabulary for the first term. Ask them directly and they will answer, sincerely and without noticing, in terms of the second.

You can watch this happen in real time on any political programme. It is the water they swim in.

## It Captures Things That Have Nothing To Do With Politics

The mechanism does not stay in its lane, and that is the part that should worry people who do not care about politics.

Neil deGrasse Tyson, discussing the pandemic, argued that science lost a great deal of public trust during it. He is right that the trust was lost. But the diagnosis usually offered — that scientists communicated badly, or overreached, or contradicted themselves — locates the fault in the wrong institution.

Vaccination had no business becoming a partisan identity marker. There is nothing about an immunological question that maps onto left and right. **The political system seized it anyway**, because that system's operating procedure is to convert every contested question into a red-versus-blue signal, since that conversion is what mobilises voters.

Once converted, the scientific question became unanswerable in public. Not because the science was unclear, but because any answer now carried tribal meaning. A system that does this reliably, to any live question that comes near it, is the thing that failed. Science was the bystander.

## It Reaches the Economy

There is a further cost, and it is one Sowell documents at length in *Basic Economics*.

Honesty underwrites prosperity. High-trust societies need less contract enforcement, suffer less crime, verify less, and can therefore transact more cheaply. This is not sentimentality — it shows up in transaction costs, in the price of credit, in whether strangers can do business at all. Sowell notes that a society's honesty depends on many factors, and lists the political system among them.

Follow that through. A political system built on unconstrained promises, periodic corruption scandals, and public figures who are widely known to say things they do not mean is not merely wasting public money. **It is teaching the entire population, continuously and by example, that deception is normal and effective.**

That lesson does not stay in the political sphere. It raises the cost of every deal in the economy, not only the ones government touches. The politician who lies about housing policy is imposing a tax on the plumber negotiating with a supplier three hundred miles away, who now — correctly — trusts a little less than he otherwise would.

## The Common Thread

Three different domains, one mechanism. The system does not select for people who ask what is right; it converts every question it touches into a question about winning; and it teaches, by relentless example, that saying what works beats saying what is true.

None of that is a failure of the individuals involved. That pundit was answering the question her profession had trained her to hear. The problem is the training, and the training is the system working exactly as designed.
