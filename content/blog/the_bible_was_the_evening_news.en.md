Here is an exercise. Take a stranger with no knowledge of our civilisation, hand him a random sample of two hundred Hollywood films, and ask him to describe how we live.

He will report that we are a society of murderers and thieves. That every city block hosts a shooting. That we are permanently at war, that our marriages end in betrayal, that our police are corrupt and our scientists are mad, and that an alarming fraction of us are serial killers. He would be wrong about nearly everything, and he would be wrong in a specific direction: he would have mistaken what we find *interesting* for what we *are*.

I want to apply the same exercise to the Bible.

## What We Are Told It Shows

People read the Bible as a window onto the ancient Near East, and they draw large conclusions from it. They read of child sacrifice and conclude it was widespread. They read of Abraham, of David, of Jezebel, and imagine the culture was populated by such figures and shaped by such episodes. They meet a prohibition and infer that the thing prohibited was common; they meet a hero and infer that heroism was the norm.

Scholars do some of this too, more carefully, but the popular reading is not careful at all. It treats the text as representative. It is not representative. It was never meant to be.

## What It Actually Was

Before print, before theatre in most of the region, before anything we would recognise as news, the stories that became the Bible were what people had. They were recited, sung, performed at festivals, retold at night. They were the entertainment, and they competed for attention the way entertainment always competes: by being more extreme than life.

Look at what makes the cut. Floods that drown the world. Cities destroyed by fire from the sky. A father asked to slaughter his son. Plagues, sieges, massacres, a king dancing naked, a prophet swallowed by a fish. The genealogies are boring, and everyone skips them, and they are almost the only parts that read like a record rather than a performance.

**A collection selected for its power to hold an audience tells you what held an audience.** It does not tell you what an ordinary Tuesday was like, for the same reason that our films do not.

## The Selection Effect, Named

This is a selection effect and it is worth calling it that, because the same error appears everywhere stories are used as evidence.

Our novels, our films, our news are overwhelmingly composed of the extreme cases: the crime, the disaster, the outlier. That is not a distortion introduced by cynical editors. It is what stories are for. A story about a normal day is not a story. So any corpus of stories, from any culture, will over-represent violence, betrayal, catastrophe, and larger-than-life character, in proportion to how much of it the audience could bear.

Ancient Israel was a small agricultural society whose people mostly farmed, herded, married, paid taxes to whoever was collecting them that century, and died of ordinary things. Almost none of that is in the text, because none of it would have kept anyone awake by the fire.

## Two Corrections That Follow

**First, do not read frequency from the text.** That the Bible mentions child sacrifice tells you that child sacrifice was a thing the audience found horrifying and gripping, which is a fact about the audience. How common it was is a question for archaeology, and archaeology's answer is: rare, and mostly attributed to neighbours.

**Second, do not read influence from the text.** We know these characters because the book that carried them was later canonised and copied for two millennia. That is a fact about the book's afterlife, not about the standing of Jezebel in her own century. A prototypical character in a surviving story is not evidence of a prototypical person in a vanished society.

## Why the Error Persists

Partly because the text arrives with a claim of authority that films do not make. Nobody thinks a thriller is a census. But the Bible has been presented for so long as a record, in the plain sense, that reading it as its own culture's entertainment feels disrespectful, when it is simply accurate.

And partly because it is more fun. A past full of prophets and slaughter is a better past to read about than one full of barley. The same appetite that selected these stories in the first place selects, in us, the reading that takes them at face value.

The correction is not to read the Bible less. It is to read it the way you would read our films if you wanted to know about us: as evidence of what a people wanted to hear, which is real evidence, and interesting, and not the same thing as evidence of how they lived.
