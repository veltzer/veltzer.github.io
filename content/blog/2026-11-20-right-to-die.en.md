When secular people talk about religious coercion, they usually mean the
obvious things: who can marry whom, what's open on a Saturday, who controls
burials. But the deepest religious coercion imposed on non-believers, in my
view, is one most people never notice: the idea that your life isn't yours to
end.

## Where the idea comes from

The belief that a person may not choose their own death didn't come out of
nowhere. It comes almost entirely from religion. In Judaism, Christianity, and
Islam, life belongs to God. You hold it on loan, and ending it yourself is a
sin, in some traditions one of the gravest. For centuries suicides were denied
proper burial, their property was confiscated, and survivors were punished for
the dead person's "crime."

Most of those cruelties are gone. The underlying idea isn't. It survives in
laws against assisting a death, in the way medical systems treat a competent
adult's wish to die as a symptom to be managed rather than a decision to be
respected, and in public debate, where the assumption that life must be
preserved at any cost is rarely questioned out loud.

## Let's be blunt

Luckily for us, in real life there is no God, and there is no sin. Nobody lent
you your life, and nobody is waiting to collect it back on their own schedule.
Your life belongs to you, the person who lives it. Not to a deity, not to the
rabbinate or the church, not to your neighbours, and not to the state.

Once you take God out of the picture, the religious argument collapses. What's
left is a set of rules written for believers and enforced on everyone.

## Having a right isn't the same as using it

Let me be clear about what I'm not saying. I'm not for suicide. I would urge
almost anyone, and especially someone who isn't seriously ill, not to do it.
Most despair passes, and most people who survive a crisis are glad they did.

But wanting people not to do something is different from denying them the
right to do it. We defend free speech for opinions we despise. We let adults
smoke, drink, climb mountains, and make terrible financial decisions. We do it
because a free society treats competent adults as the owners of their own
lives, mistakes included. The final decision about one's own life should be no
exception.

This matters most at the end. If my health ever collapsed badly, I'd want this
option to be real, legal, and dignified, not something I had to beg for.

## Safeguards, not gatekeepers

I'm not against a process. A doctor or a committee confirming that the person
is a competent adult, acting freely, and not in a passing crisis is
reasonable. What I object to is a process that interrogates, judges whether
your reasons are good enough, or quietly imposes someone else's values on your
decision. A safeguard protects the person's choice. A gatekeeper replaces it.

## The other side

There are serious secular objections too, and they deserve an answer. Many
people who want to die are in treatable depression, and the wish often passes.
Critics worry that elderly, disabled, or poor people could feel pressure to
stop being a "burden." Doctors' groups argue that helping patients die changes
the nature of medicine. And some point to places like Canada and the
Netherlands, where eligibility has steadily widened, as evidence of a slippery
slope. My answer is that these are arguments for good safeguards, not for
keeping a religious prohibition in place. They're still arguments worth taking
seriously.
