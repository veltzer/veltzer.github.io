I have written a lot about how badly people are connected to reality:
[whether we are getting better at it](@/blog/2019-10-25-connected-to-reality.md),
and [why most of our institutions pull us away from it](@/blog/2026-11-20-untethered-from-reality.md).
Every one of those posts assumes something I never argued for: that being
connected to reality is worth having in the first place. One of them even ends
by admitting it. This post is the missing argument.

It isn't an obvious point. A believer who is comforted by heaven, a patriot
who is proud of an invented history, a man who is sure his horoscope explains
his week: they all seem to be doing fine. So why should any of them bother?

## Reality collects its debts

The first answer is the practical one. Actions run on beliefs. If the beliefs
are true, the actions tend to work. If they're false, they work only by luck.
The bridge doesn't care what the engineer believed, and the tumour doesn't
care what the patient hoped.

The standard reply is that most false beliefs never get tested this way. You
can believe in astrology your whole life and never pay for it. That's true of
any single belief, and it misses how beliefs work. You don't know in advance
which of your beliefs you'll have to act on. The person who trusts stories
over evidence about the stars is the same person who will one day choose
between a doctor and a healer, or between a pension fund and a friend with a
sure thing. Beliefs aren't kept in separate boxes. What you carry from one to
the next is the habit: do I check, or do I believe what feels good? A habit
can't be careless on Monday and rigorous on Tuesday.

## Other people pay for your beliefs

The second answer is that beliefs aren't private. You vote with them. You
raise children with them. You decide with them whether to vaccinate, whom to
fear, which wars to support, and what to demand from the people who govern
you. A false belief about how the economy works costs you little, since your
single vote changes nothing. Multiplied by millions it becomes policy, and
everyone lives under it.

This is why "it makes me happy" is a weak defence. It may well make you happy.
The bill goes to somebody else.

## A false map isn't your life

The third answer is the one I care about most. Suppose a comfortable illusion
really did cost nothing, to you or to anyone. Would it be fine then?

The philosopher Robert Nozick asked this with a thought experiment. Imagine a
machine that gives you any experience you want, perfectly, for the rest of
your life: love, success, adventure, all of it felt as real. Would you plug
in for good? Almost nobody says yes. People want to actually do things, not
to feel as if they did them. They want to be loved by a real person, not to
have the sensation of it.

That refusal tells us something. We don't only want pleasant experiences. We
want our lives to be about something that is actually there. A person whose
central beliefs are false is living a milder version of the machine. Their
choices are made for a world that doesn't exist, which means they aren't
really their choices. Whoever supplied the story is making them. That is the
connection between truth and freedom: you can only decide for yourself if you
know what you're deciding about.

## The comfort is on loan

There is one more thing wrong with the comfortable illusion: it fails exactly
when you need it. A belief that was never tested is fine in good times. Then
comes the illness, the death, the betrayal, and the story meets the one thing
it can't absorb. Some people double down. Many lose both the comfort and the
years they could have spent building something sturdier. A view of the world
that already includes death and bad luck has less to lose when they arrive.

## What I can't prove

Here is the honest limit of the argument. I can't prove, from nothing, that
truth is better than comfort. If a person fully understood the trade, harmed
nobody, and still chose the illusion, I couldn't show they made a logical
error. Values don't follow from facts alone.

But notice two things. First, almost nobody is that person, because nobody
can choose an illusion with open eyes: once you know it's an illusion it stops
working. People don't pick the comfortable story. They're handed it, usually
as children. Second, even the most committed believer in comfort wants a
tethered pilot, a tethered surgeon, and a tethered engineer. Everybody already
agrees that reality matters when the stakes are theirs and the feedback is
fast. All I'm asking is to be consistent about it.

## The other side

There is real evidence that some illusions help. Psychologists have found that
mildly depressed people sometimes judge their own abilities more accurately
than healthy people do, and that a bit of unearned optimism goes with better
health and more persistence. William James argued that in some matters, like
whether a friendship or a hard project will succeed, believing ahead of the
evidence helps make the thing true. And for a dying person, it is fair to ask
what an accurate picture of the universe is actually good for. I think these
are arguments about dosage and timing, not about direction. They're still the
best objections there are, and they should be answered rather than waved away.
