No grown adult believes in Santa Claus. If a 30-year-old told you, sincerely, that a man in a red suit visits every house on Earth in one night, you wouldn't call it a deeply held conviction. You'd call it what it is. Yet millions of adults believe something just as extraordinary: that the creator of a hundred billion galaxies became a first-century Galilean rabbi, was executed by Rome, and then got up and walked out of his tomb. We don't laugh at that. We build cathedrals to it.

This post is about that double standard, and about why I think believing these stories as an adult is not a harmless quirk. It is stupid, and adults who do it are being stupid.

## The story doesn't survive a moment of honest scrutiny

Start with scale. We now know that Earth is a speck orbiting an ordinary star, in an ordinary galaxy, one of roughly 10^11 galaxies. Every major discovery of the last five centuries has pushed humans further from the center: Copernicus, Darwin, Hubble. And against all of that, Christianity asks us to believe that the whole cosmos turns on one execution in a Roman province.

Now AI has pushed us off one more pedestal. Human intelligence was supposed to be the last frontier, the thing that made us special in all that emptiness. Machines now match or beat us at tasks we once thought defined a mind: chess, Go, protein folding, passing professional exams, writing. Each of those lines was crossed with little more than a news cycle, and life went on. If even our minds aren't the crown of creation, a story that puts us at the center of the cosmos looks smaller still.

Psychology has delivered the same blow from the inside. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky spent decades cataloguing how badly human judgment fails: confirmation bias, motivated reasoning, overconfidence, the hunger to see patterns and agents where there are none. We are not the rational crown of creation. We are anxious primates who believe what comforts us and then invent reasons for it afterward. That is exactly the kind of mind that would invent a god who loves it and a resurrection that saves it.

Some will say these biases infect everyone, atheists included. True, but that misses the point. Science exists precisely because individual minds can't be trusted. Peer review, replication and controlled experiments are machinery built to catch bias that no single person could catch alone. An atheist who grounds their worldview in scientific findings inherits those corrections, so their beliefs about the world are less biased. A theist grounds theirs in revelation and tradition, where doubt is a sin and conviction is a virtue, and nothing catches the errors. That is the real difference between them: one side borrows a bias-correcting method, the other enshrines the bias.

This isn't a story that grew out of evidence. It's a story that grew out of need. A movement's leader was publicly crucified. His followers had staked everything on him. Rather than admit they were wrong, they told themselves he wasn't really dead. Psychologists have a name for this: cognitive dissonance. Groups whose prophecies fail often double down instead of giving up. The resurrection is the oldest and most successful example.

## Faith is designed to dodge the test

Ask a believer for a test, and you'll hear that God must not be tested. How convenient. The one claim that would settle everything is the one claim declared off-limits.

And where tests have been run, they've failed. The largest study of intercessory prayer, the 2006 STEP trial, found that prayed-for heart surgery patients did no better than anyone else. Those who knew they were being prayed for did slightly worse.

Watch how believers actually live. They pray, then they go to the doctor. They say death is a doorway to paradise, then they grieve and fight to stay alive like everyone else. Their actions tell you how much they really trust the story.

## The real motive is fear

Strip away the theology and what's left is fear of death. Research in terror management theory shows that reminding people of their mortality makes them cling harder to belief in an afterlife. The resurrection isn't a historical conclusion. It's a comfort blanket wearing the costume of history.

I understand fear of death. Everyone has it. What I don't respect is pretending that fear is evidence.

## The worst part: making bullshit sacred

Humans have always believed nonsense. Religion's unique achievement was making nonsense sacred. Once a claim is sacred, questioning it becomes sin, doubt becomes a moral failure, and criticism becomes rudeness. Science does the opposite. It treats nothing as sacred, which is exactly why it can correct its mistakes. Two thousand years later, Christianity still recites essentially the same creed. Name one other field of human knowledge that has made zero progress in two millennia and is still proud of it.

And sacredness came with enforcement. Thomas Aikenhead was hanged in Scotland in 1697, at 20, for blasphemy. In Poland, a nobleman was executed in 1689 for writing that God does not exist. Today, apostasy can still bring a death sentence in some countries. For centuries, nonbelievers were the ones who were supposed to feel ashamed.

## Every believer is part of the enterprise

The moderate believer will say: I'm not one of those people. But by reciting the creed, filling the pews, and teaching the story to children before they can evaluate it, every believer lends respectability to the system that shields the worst of it. Superstition, anti-intellectualism, and contempt for evidence all draw strength from the idea that faith is a virtue.

The data points the same way. A 2013 meta-analysis by Miron Zuckerman and colleagues, pooling 63 studies, found a reliable negative correlation between religiosity and intelligence. Believers score lower on average. But raw IQ isn't even the point. Stupidity isn't only a lack of intelligence; it's what you let your intelligence serve. A clever mind that hands its judgment over to a story written thousands of years ago, and then spends its brilliance defending that story, is being stupid with extra steps.

A child who believes in Santa has an excuse. An adult with access to every library, every counterargument, and every scientific discovery of the last 500 years does not. At 30, believing that a dead man rose from his grave isn't innocence. It's stupidity, plain and simple.

## It's time to stop pretending

I'm not asking for laws against belief. I'm asking for honesty. We should stop treating these stories as profound simply because they are old and widely held. We should stop giving them a respect we'd give to no other unsupported claim. And believers, if they value integrity, should turn on their own faith the same scrutiny they would turn on Zeus, Thor, or Santa Claus.

They'll find the same thing. There's nothing there.
