We are not wired for raw data, probability distributions, or complex abstractions. Our brains crave narrative — it is our primary mechanism for understanding the world and making sense of our existence. When presented with a choice between a dry statistical reality and a compelling story, the story routinely wins. We map value and morality onto characters, using plots to navigate social hierarchies and predict human behaviour. This cognitive disposition was a powerful evolutionary tool in a small tribal world, but it poses a unique danger in the modern age: **because a compelling narrative feels right to our minds, for humans, stories equal truth.** That equation allows falsehoods to masquerade as fundamental truths simply because they are well told.

## The Evolutionary Trap: Why a Story Feels Like an Explanation

As I have argued when examining why [truth is never short while falsehoods spread](@/blog/brevity_is_not_truth.md) and how [the small budget of human working memory shapes what we call beautiful](@/blog/beauty_truth_and_working_memory.md), the real causes of almost anything important are long, statistical, and conditional. A real social or historical outcome is a weighted sum of dozens of interacting factors, none of them dramatic on its own and none of them reducible to a single hero or villain.

A human brain does not want a weighted sum. It wants a protagonist, a motive, a conflict, and an arc. When a sequence of events is packaged into that shape, it slips effortlessly through our cognitive window and produces the warm sensation of understanding. **We mistake that cognitive ease for evidence.** If the story coheres emotionally, we treat it as true; if an accurate explanation refuses to cohere into a story, we treat it as unconvincing or incomplete.

## How Teaching Children Stories Corrupts Critical Thinking

Nowhere is this clearer — or more damaging — than in how we raise children. Culture treats storytelling to children as an unquestioned good. In reality, **when we feed children stories, we train their minds to prefer comforting illusions over demanding truths.**

Children are evolutionarily designed to absorb the frameworks adults hand them during their formative years, a vulnerability I have discussed in the context of [childhood indoctrination](@/blog/childhood_indoctrination.md) and [the simplifying myths school teaches children](@/blog/why_school_lies_to_children.md). Long before a child is taught what a distribution is, or how to check a claim against evidence, we saturate their mind with fables, fairy tales, and parables. Look at what those stories actually teach:

- **A simplistic binary of good and evil.** Fables divide the world into pure heroes and pure villains, conditioning the subconscious to flinch from moral and causal complexity.
- **Single-motive causation.** Characters act from one legible trait — greed, courage, jealousy, kindness — rather than the messy, multi-factor pressures that govern real human behaviour.
- **Guaranteed moral resolution.** Every plot bends toward a tidy ending in which the lesson is clear and the ledger is balanced, installing the expectation that reality itself is arranged around a moral arc.

By imposing narrative arcs onto human behaviour and social structures during a child's most impressionable years, we actively inhibit their capacity for nuanced, objective critical thinking later in life. We install a defective epistemic operating system first, and then wonder why empirical reasoning struggles to run on it later.

## The Adult Consequence: Rejecting Ambiguity and Following Charismatics

A child trained on stories grows into an adult with a permanent emotional dependency on narrative resolution.

When that adult confronts real-world problems — where evidence is noisy, trade-offs have no clean moral winner, and outcomes are probabilistic — they reject the ambiguity. Reality feels broken to them because it lacks a plot. And because [literature and art train the public to think through vivid individual cases rather than populations](@/blog/literature_and_stories_make_the_public_stupid.md), adults routinely accept a single moving anecdote in place of data, confusing [emotional consolation with evidence](@/blog/consolation_is_not_evidence.md).

Worse, this dependency fosters an unhealthy reliance on charismatic figures. Demagogues, political leaders, pundits, and ideological gurus succeed not by offering critical analysis of facts, but by presenting entire worldviews disguised as epic tales. They supply a cast of villains to blame, a crisis with a single cause, and a promised redemption. To a public conditioned from infancy to equate a gripping arc with truth, the charismatic storyteller always beats the person holding a spreadsheet.

## Capitalism Works Against Truth

If human psychology is the demand side of this problem, **capitalism is the engine on the supply side — and here, capitalism actively works against truth.**

Capitalism does not force creators at gunpoint, but it relentlessly pushes them through incentives. Writers, directors, poets, journalists, and content creators compete in a market where survival and reward depend on capturing human attention. Because commercial success is tied to mass appeal, capitalism incentivizes creators to produce stories that appeal to people's emotional biases rather than factual reality.

And here is the structural catch: stories that appeal to people are rarely true. Truth is dry, qualified, slow, and indifferent to what flatters the audience. An appealing story is fast, dramatic, binary, and emotionally validating. When creators are pushed by the market to maximize engagement, truth is systematically sacrificed for profitability. The result is an industrial marketplace of comforting falsehoods, where the most skilled storytellers in society are paid handsomely to reinforce the very cognitive biases that blind us.

To protect its own market viability, this commercial ecosystem goes one step further: it frames objective truth as cynical, cold, or boring. Careful quantitative analysis is dismissed as joyless bean-counting, while sweeping fictional narratives are celebrated as "deep" human truths. That framing keeps societal focus locked on consuming engaging fictions rather than critically examining real-world structures.

## Our Most Important Battles Are With Our Own Nature

It is tempting to look at this machinery and conclude that the enemy is merely the storyteller or the market. We are indeed fighting capitalism's incentives here, and we are fighting a culture that canonizes [art that teaches the wrong way to think](@/blog/art_teaches_the_wrong_way_to_think.md). But the market only sells what our biology buys.

Ultimately, our greatest struggle is not against external forces or societal systems, but against the ancient contours of our own minds. As I have argued across this blog — whether about [pushing people to act against their instincts](@/blog/forcing_people_against_nature.md) or using [social design to counter our genetic defaults](@/blog/two_levers_genes_and_society.md) — **our most important battles are with our own nature.**

Overcoming our evolutionary predisposition toward narrative simplification and emotional biases — **the key to survival** — is the defining battle for preserving objective truth in the modern age. A species armed with modern technology cannot afford to govern itself by campfire tales. Survival requires recognizing that a good story is almost never the truth, and building the intellectual discipline to demand the facts precisely when the story feels right.
