By reducing complex data to a single emotional proxy, modern media actively avoids statistical truth. They rely on the human angle not to illuminate, but because they bank on the public's statistical illiteracy. This creates a self-reinforcing loop: by substituting anecdote for evidence, they confirm biases and maintain engagement by keeping the audience comfortable in their misapprehension, effectively **trading reality for a more palatable fiction.**

## The Single Emotional Proxy

Almost every question that matters to a society is a statistical question. Is a drug safe? Is crime rising? Does a policy help the people it claims to help? Are immigrants, or the police, or teenagers on their phones, more dangerous than they used to be? None of these has an answer in the form of a person. Each has an answer in the form of a rate, a trend, a distribution and a comparison against a baseline.

Watch how such a question arrives on the screen. It arrives as one face: the mother whose child reacted badly to a vaccine, the shopkeeper who was robbed twice, the family that fell through the cracks of a welfare programme. The face is real, and the suffering is real. What is false is the implied claim that the face *stands for* the data. One case tells you that something can happen. It tells you nothing about how often it happens, whether that is more or less than before, or whether the alternative is worse. A single emotional proxy is not a small sample of the truth; it is a replacement for it.

## Why the Human Angle Is Chosen

The standard defence is that the human angle makes abstract numbers relatable: people do not connect with a chart, so we show them a person and let the person carry the meaning. If that were the purpose, the person would come *with* the numbers — here is one family, and here is how typical their case is, and here is the trend it belongs to. That segment is almost never made.

The face is chosen *instead* of the numbers, and it is chosen because the producers know, correctly, that most of their audience cannot evaluate the numbers anyway. A viewer who does not know what a base rate is cannot ask whether three tragic cases out of ten million doses is alarming. A viewer who confuses an absolute risk with a relative one cannot tell that "doubles the risk" may mean going from one in a million to two in a million. A viewer who has never heard of regression to the mean will credit whatever intervention followed an unusually bad year. As I argued in [The News Is Not Trying to Inform You](@/blog/the_news_is_not_trying_to_inform_you.md), the format is built for catharsis, not understanding. **The statistical illiteracy of the public is not an obstacle the media work around; it is the ground they build on.**

## The Self-Reinforcing Loop

This would be bad enough if it were a one-time failure. It is worse, because it compounds.

1. **The audience arrives with a bias.** It already believes crime is rising, or that some group is dangerous, or that a technology is poisoning children.
2. **The media supply an anecdote that fits.** There is always one: in a country of millions, every rare event happens somewhere, every week.
3. **The anecdote feels like confirmation.** It is vivid, it has a face, and it agrees with what the viewer already thought — which, as I discussed in [For Humans, Stories Equals Truth](@/blog/for_humans_stories_equals_truth.md), is exactly the sensation the mind mistakes for evidence.
4. **Engagement rises.** The viewer stays, shares, comes back tomorrow for more of the same.
5. **The outlet learns.** Whatever kept the audience watching is produced again, so the bias is fed again, and grows.

At no point in this loop does anyone have to lie. Every anecdote can be accurate. The distortion is entirely in the *selection*: which true cases are shown, and the decision never to show the denominator. A diet of accurate anecdotes chosen to please can leave an audience more wrong than it would be with no news at all.

## Comfort as the Product

The audience is not merely misinformed; it is kept *comfortable* in its misinformation. A real statistical picture is often unsettling in a quiet, undramatic way: the thing you feared is rare, the thing you ignored is common, your side's favourite policy has no measurable effect, the villain of the story is a distribution and not a person. That picture offers no one to be angry at and nothing to feel righteous about. It asks the viewer to hold uncertainty and to change their mind.

The anecdote asks for neither. It hands over a clear villain, a clear victim, and a feeling that one already understood the world correctly. That feeling is what is being sold, and the price is reality. The trade is invisible precisely because the fiction is built entirely out of true parts. As with [the brevity that lets falsehood outrun truth](@/blog/brevity_is_not_truth.md), the comfortable version wins not because it is persuasive on the merits but because it costs the mind less.

## What Would Honest Reporting Look Like

It is not hard to describe, which is part of what makes its absence telling. Every human story would come attached to its denominator: how many cases, out of how many, compared to what, over what period. Every risk would be stated in absolute terms. Every "rising" or "falling" would come with the long-run trend, not the last two data points. The face could still be shown — it just would not be allowed to stand in for the numbers.

By the [cruel metric I proposed for journalism](@/blog/a_cruel_metric_for_journalism.md), almost no coverage passes, and this is a large part of why. But the responsibility does not end with the media. An outlet can only sell anecdote-for-evidence to an audience that cannot tell the difference. **Basic statistical literacy — base rates, absolute versus relative risk, sampling, regression to the mean — is not an academic luxury. It is the only defence a citizen has against being governed by the most vivid story of the week.** Until it is common, the loop will keep turning, and the media will keep finding it profitable to keep it turning.
