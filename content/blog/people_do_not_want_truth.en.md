Harari argues in several places that people cannot attain real happiness until they know the truth about their lives. It is a flattering claim and I think it is false — and getting it wrong matters, because any programme for connecting people to reality has to start from an accurate account of how much they want it.

## The Counterevidence Is Everywhere

People like delusion. They will spend entire lives inside one, defend it against evidence, and organise their communities around it.

Religion is the standing proof. Billions of people hold beliefs that conflict with what is known, and the holding is not reluctant or embarrassed. It is central, cherished, and passed to children deliberately. **If people wanted the truth, this would not be the most common configuration of a human life.**

## Where Truth-Seeking Is Real

There is a genuine appetite for truth, but it is narrow and instrumental.

You want to know whether that shape in the grass is a tiger or a poodle, because getting it wrong can kill you. You want to know whether the bridge holds, whether the food is spoiled, whether the person you are dealing with has cheated others.

**These are cases where being wrong has fast, personal, unavoidable consequences.** In that class we are excellent — genuinely impressive at inference, cautious about evidence, quick to update.

Outside that class, the appetite disappears. People are not interested in the truth about the origin of the universe, or the causes of a war two centuries ago, or whether their political convictions survive contact with data. They are interested in what makes them comfortable, and there is no penalty for being wrong.

## Why the Instinct Works This Way

This is not a defect, which is what makes it hard to argue with.

Tracking truth is expensive. It costs attention, and it frequently costs you the comfort of a belief that was doing useful work — holding a community together, making a death bearable, justifying a life already lived.

Where error is punished quickly, that cost is worth paying and we evolved to pay it. Where error costs nothing for decades or forever, the calculation reverses. **A creature that pursued truth in domains with no feedback would be paying a real price for no return.**

So the pattern is not human weakness. It is the correct response to the incentive structure, and I suspect any evolved creature would show it.

## Why Harari's Error Matters

If you believe people want the truth, then the obstacle is access — and the remedy is information. Explain better, publish more, correct the record.

We have run that experiment at enormous scale. Information has never been more available, and the results are not what the theory predicted. The comfortable falsehood is as widely held as ever, and now better organised.

**If people mostly do not want the truth outside the domains where error hurts, the problem is not access. It is motivation**, and information does not supply motivation.

Which points at a different remedy, and I think a more promising one: rather than trying to make people want the truth in general, extend the conditions under which wanting it is rational. Shorten the feedback. Make error visible and personal. Attach consequences to being wrong in domains that currently have none.

That is much harder than publishing another explainer. But it addresses the actual mechanism, rather than a flattering story about what people are like.
