We like to think ideas live or die on their merits: true ideas spread, false ones fade, good ones win out over bad ones. But there's a force acting on ideas that doesn't care about any of that. The market responds to one thing only, money. Whatever an idea's truth or virtue, if it costs money, the market pushes against it. It doesn't always kill the idea. More often it does something quieter: it takes the bite out of it.

## Defanging a bad idea

Thomas Sowell likes to point to an example from the American South. Drawing on the work of historian Jennifer Roback, he notes that streetcar companies there resisted laws forcing them to segregate their passengers. These weren't civil rights crusaders. Separate cars and sections simply cost money, and the companies didn't want to pay for them.

Some of those executives may well have been racists themselves. It doesn't matter. Their personal beliefs counted for less than the balance sheet. Racism had a price, and the market pushed against it.

It's a comforting story, and it's often told as proof that markets are a force for good. But it only shows that markets are a force against costs. Here the cost happened to be a bad idea.

## Defanging a good idea

Here's the other side, from my own experience. I was teaching software development at a company run by ultra-Orthodox Jews. At one point I explained that software development, done right, is a lot like evolution. That's what agile is: making small improvements, each of which keeps the software working, just as evolution makes small changes, each of which keeps the organism alive.

I could hear the reaction in the room: sighs of discomfort, a wave of the hand, quiet objection. My students didn't believe in evolution. Then they complained, and my company told me to stop making the comparison.

So I stopped. Not because the analogy was wrong. It's a correct and useful way to understand agile. I stopped because the client was paying, and the idea was costing my employer goodwill. The market defanged a true idea exactly the way it defanged a false one.

## Blind to truth and morality alike

It's tempting to describe this as the market being morally blind, and sometimes that's exactly what it is. The streetcar story is a moral one: segregation is a moral wrong, and the market happened to work against it.

But not everything the market defangs is a moral question. Comparing agile to evolution has no moral implications at all. It's simply true or false, useful or not. The market was just as indifferent to that.

That's the real point. The market isn't biased toward good ideas or bad ones, true ones or false ones, moral ones or immoral ones. It has no view on any of it. It pushes every idea in one direction only, toward whatever costs less and pays more. Sometimes that lines up with what's right or true, and sometimes it doesn't, purely by chance.

## No easy fix

I don't have a solution. Changing how the market works is about as hard as it gets. But it's worth seeing the force for what it is. When an idea succeeds in the market, that tells you it's profitable, not that it's true or good. And when an idea gets quietly dropped, it may not have been wrong. It may just have been expensive.

## A counterpoint: silenced by whoever pays

Calling the market "blind" may give it too much credit for neutrality. In my classroom, the market didn't silence the evolution analogy on its own. The pressure came from my clients and their beliefs, with their money behind them. The idea was suppressed because the people paying didn't like it.

Seen this way, the market silences ideas according to who holds the money. An idea doesn't need to be false or harmful to be pushed out. It only needs to bother the people paying the bills. In the streetcar story, segregation was the idea that cost money, so it was the one that got suppressed. But where customers will pay for discrimination, economists from Gary Becker on have noted that the market can just as easily suppress the opposition to it.

This doesn't overturn the thesis. It sharpens it. The market has no view of its own on truth or morality, but it isn't neutral either. It quiets whatever the people with money don't want to hear.
