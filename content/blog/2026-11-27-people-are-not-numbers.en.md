*In defense of compression: why rejecting statistics means rejecting knowledge.*

I was in a discussion with some Wikipedia editors when someone cited a survey about public support for a government. One of the editors objected. Reality is not binary, he said. People don't simply support their government or not support it. Quoting the survey, in his view, reduced a complicated reality to a binary condition, and that was wrong.

Notice what he did not say. He did not say the survey was badly run, or the sample was too small, or the question was loaded. He said the very idea of measuring support for a government is illegitimate.

That argument comes in many costumes, and I hear it from very intelligent people. I want to take it apart, because underneath the costumes it is always the same argument, and it is always wrong.

## Statistics is compression

Here is what the editor doesn't understand. Statistics is a way to compress a complicated piece of information into a shorter one.

Ten million people each have their own nuanced, shifting, half-formed opinion of their government. Nobody can hold ten million opinions in their head. A survey compresses them into a few numbers you can actually think with. Of course information is lost. That is not a flaw of the method. That is the method.

And that is not special to statistics. That is what knowledge is. Every piece of understanding we have is a compression of something more complicated into something simpler. A map leaves out almost everything about the territory, and that's exactly why you can use it to find your way. A physical law compresses countless observations into one line. An average height tells you nothing about any single person and a great deal about a population. If losing detail made a summary illegitimate, we would have to give up maps, laws, averages, models, and every other kind of knowledge along with surveys.

The editor's specific complaint doesn't even hold on its own terms. Plenty of surveys aren't binary at all. They ask people to rate their support on a scale, from strongly support to strongly oppose. But that's beside the point. Even a binary question is a perfectly good compression, as long as you know what it compresses.

## A survey can be wrong, and that's a different question

None of this means every survey is right. A survey can be wrong in many ways. The sample may be too small or unrepresentative. The question may be worded to push people toward an answer. The surveyor may be biased and play around with the numbers. There may be outright corruption.

All of that is absolutely fine to raise, because all of it can be checked. You can look at the sample, the wording, the methodology, who paid for it, and whether other surveys agree. A specific criticism of a specific survey is how statistics is supposed to work.

But that is not what the editor was saying. He was saying that surveying support for a government is inherently wrong, whatever the method. That is not a critique of a survey. It is a rejection of the whole idea of measuring.

## The many disguises

The argument shows up in a lot of forms. They look different, but they are all the same argument: the numbers can't be trusted, so I'll trust something else.

**"Reality is not binary."** True, and no survey claims it is. A survey compresses reality, it doesn't replace it.

**"People are not numbers."** Sure, people are not numbers. But when you want to understand what is going on with ten million of them, you have to go to statistics. There is simply no other way. What are we going to go with, hunches?

**"You're too analytical."** I've heard this one from very smart people. You only look at numbers, why don't you look at anecdotes, why don't you use your feelings more? But feelings and anecdotes are not an alternative way of knowing about ten million people. They are a worse way.

**"Correlation isn't causation."** This one is actually true, which makes it the sneakiest of the lot. Correlation isn't causation. But a survey only ever shows correlation, and no one is claiming otherwise. If you want causation, you run more studies and more tests. A true statement is being used as a dodge against a claim nobody made.

**"You can prove anything with statistics."** You can certainly mislead with bad statistics. The way you catch it is with better statistics, not by giving up on numbers and trusting your gut, which is far easier to mislead.

**"Lies, damned lies, and statistics."** The famous line, popularized by Mark Twain, is a nice joke and a terrible epistemology. It lumps the tool together with its misuse.

**"That doesn't match my experience."** Your experience is a sample. A tiny, unrandom one.

**"Every case is unique."** Every case is unique, and patterns still exist across them. Medicine works because of this. Every patient is unique, and drugs are still tested on thousands of them.

**"Numbers can't measure what really matters"** and **"the numbers miss the human story."** The human story of one person is real and worth telling. It tells you nothing about how common that story is. Only counting can.

## What you get instead

So what is the alternative to statistics? It isn't nuance. When you reject the survey, you don't get a richer picture of reality. You get whatever picture was already in your head.

Often that is anecdote. You generalize from the five people you happen to know, which is just a terrible survey with a sample size of five. Psychologists call one version of this the availability bias: we judge how common something is by how easily examples come to mind, our friends, a vivid news story, the loudest voices online. Statistics is exactly the tool built to correct for that.

Often it is something worse. Group loyalty, personal identity, political affiliation. If the survey says your side is losing support, the easiest move is to declare that surveys can't capture reality, and keep believing you know the real situation. I wrote about how far people will go to protect their group and their identity in [Why the Faithful Lie](@/blog/2026-11-27-why-the-faithful-lie.md). "Reality is not binary" can be a very respectable-sounding cover for exactly that.

Either way, the person rejecting statistics hasn't escaped reductionism. They have swapped an explicit compression, one you can inspect, criticize, and correct, for a hidden one that no one can check.

## The cure for bad statistics

The answer to a bad survey is not no survey. It is a better survey. The answer to statistics you don't trust is more statistics, done right: bigger samples, better questions, open methods, independent replication.

Knowledge is compression. You can argue about whether a particular compression is any good, and you should. But once you reject compression itself, you haven't defended nuance. You've given up on knowing anything at all.
