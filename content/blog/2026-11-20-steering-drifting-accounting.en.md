A few years ago I asked
[whether humanity is steering or drifting](@/blog/2020-08-21-steering-or-drifting.md):
on the line from full conscious control of our future to none, where are we?
I sketched three domains, the economy, government and the direction of
history, and guessed at a pattern: we have traded the ability to steer for
safety from bad steering, one sensible decision at a time, and nobody ever
added up the total.

I also admitted that the real work was the accounting I hadn't done. Here it
is, for six more domains. The question for each is the same: does this
increase or decrease our collective ability to decide something and then do
it?

## Law

Law looks like the steering wheel itself. It's how a society tells itself what
to do. In practice it behaves more like sediment. Statutes pile up and are
rarely repealed. Courts are bound by earlier courts. Constitutions are made
hard to change on purpose, and judges can strike down what a majority just
decided. Every one of those features protects somebody from an abuse that
really happened.

The result is that each generation inherits more rules it didn't write and
can't easily remove. Law is a ratchet: easy to add, hard to take away.

**Verdict: less steering.** The past holds more and more of the wheel.

## Science funding

Nobody decides what humanity should find out next. Money goes to thousands of
separate projects, picked by committees of scientists judging each other's
proposals. That's deliberate, and mostly right, since nobody knows where the
next discovery is.

The exceptions are worth looking at. The Manhattan Project, Apollo, and the
rush for a COVID vaccine show that science can be aimed, fast, when a
government declares an emergency and pays for it. We know how to steer here.
We do it only when frightened.

**Verdict: drift by default, steering by exception.**

## Media

To decide anything together, a society first has to look at the same thing at
the same time. For a few decades it could. A handful of newspapers and
broadcasters set a shared agenda. They did it badly and with plenty of bias,
but it was shared.

That's gone. Attention is now handed out by feeds built to keep each person
scrolling, and what each person sees is different. No editor chooses the
agenda, and the algorithm has no opinion about what matters. It was built to
hold attention, and that's all it does.

**Verdict: much less steering,** and the sharpest drop of the six.

## Education

Education is the odd one. Here the state really does steer: school is
compulsory, and someone writes the curriculum. If there's one place where we
decide what the next generation will be like, it's this.

And yet the classroom, the timetable, the subjects and the exams are
recognisably those of the nineteenth century. Curricula change by adding, not
by rethinking. Everyone with a stake can block a change and no one can force
one.

**Verdict: there is a wheel, and nobody is turning it.**

## Corporate structure

A corporation is the best steering machine we've built. It sets a goal,
assigns people, measures results and corrects course. Inside the firm there
is nothing feather-like at all.

But look at who chooses the goal. Ownership is spread across millions of
shareholders, most of whom hold the company through a fund and don't know they
own it. Managers answer for one number. So the most capable organisations on
earth are pointed, by design, at a target that no person picked, and they
aren't free to aim at anything else.

**Verdict: superb steering in the small, drift in the large.**

## International institutions

Our biggest problems are the size of the planet. Our decisions are the size of
a country. Between countries there are treaties that bind only those who sign,
a Security Council where five members can each veto anything, and no
enforcement to speak of.

Again the exceptions teach the most. Smallpox was wiped out by a coordinated
world campaign. The Montreal Protocol got the chemicals that destroy the ozone
layer phased out. Both problems were narrow, measurable, and technically
solvable, with few losers.

**Verdict: almost no steering, with two instructive successes.**

## The total

Add these to the first three and the pattern holds, with one refinement I
didn't see before.

It isn't that we can't steer. We steer very well in three situations: inside
a single organisation with a single goal, in a declared emergency, and on a
narrow technical problem where the fix is clear and few people lose. What we
can't do is steer on problems that are slow, spread out, and tangled up with
values. Those happen to be the ones that matter most.

So the trade I guessed at is real, and it has a shape. We kept the ability to
steer in the small and gave it up in the large. The parts row hard, each in
its own direction, and the whole thing floats.

I wouldn't undo the trades. Separation of powers, independent courts and free
markets were answers to real horrors. What's missing is smaller: anyone whose
job is the total. No institution asks, of each new safeguard, how much
ability to act we have left. A modest start would be laws that expire unless
renewed, and an agreed, deliberate way to declare a slow problem an emergency
before it becomes a fast one.

## The other side

The strongest objection is that drift is the point. Friedrich Hayek argued
that no central mind can hold the knowledge that is spread through a whole
society, so systems nobody steers will outperform systems somebody does. The
twentieth century offers support: the societies that steered hardest, the
Soviet Union and Mao's China, steered into famine. Meanwhile the drifting
world has become richer, healthier and longer-lived than any planned one. It
may be that what looks like floating is a very large number of small
corrections, and that this is the only kind of steering a complex
civilisation can survive. My answer is that this defends drifting on problems
that can be solved piece by piece, and says nothing about the ones that can't.
