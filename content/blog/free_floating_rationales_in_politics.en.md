Daniel Dennett has a phrase I find more useful the longer I hold it: the *free-floating rationale*. It names a reason for a design that is a perfectly good reason, that fully explains why the design is the way it is, and that **nobody ever had in mind.** Once you see how common these are in biology, you will start seeing them in government, and the second sight changes how you argue about politics.

## The Cuckoo Chick

A newly hatched cuckoo, blind and a few hours old, pushes the host's eggs out of the nest one by one. Ask why it does this and there is a clear answer: an egg that stays becomes a rival for the food the host parents bring, and a chick with no rivals gets everything. The rationale is sound. It is exactly the argument a strategist would make.

The chick has never made that argument. It cannot. It has no concept of a rival, of food, of the future. The reason exists, it did the explanatory work, and it exists nowhere in any mind. It floats free. Natural selection found the behaviour because the behaviour had that consequence, and the consequence is what we call the reason.

Biology is full of these. The plant that flowers early to beat its competitors to the pollinators does not know about competitors. The bacterium that produces a toxin to clear its neighbourhood has no neighbourhood policy. In each case there is a rationale, it is correct, and there is no rationaliser.

## Why the Concept Is Uncomfortable

We are built to find the mind behind the design. I have written about this as the [attribution error](@/blog/attribution_error_in_politics.en.md): shown a purposeful-looking outcome, we look for the person who purposed it. When we find no such person we are tempted to say there is no purpose, or to invent one.

Dennett's phrase is a third option. There *is* a purpose, in the sense that the design has a function that explains its persistence. There is *no* purposer. Both statements are true, and holding them together is a skill most people have never had to practise, because it was never needed until Darwin.

## Now Apply It to Government

Take a policy that has persisted for decades in the face of steady criticism. Farm subsidies. The tax treatment of mortgage interest. A licensing regime that keeps a profession small. Any of the arrangements that every economist across the spectrum agrees are harmful and that survive every government that promises to end them.

Ask: why does this exist? And notice the two answers you will be offered.

The first answer names a villain. Someone wanted this, someone is paid to keep it, find them and expose them. Sometimes this is even partly true. But the arrangement usually outlives everyone who might have wanted it originally, and the people currently defending it frequently did not design it and would not have.

The second answer says it is an accident, a historical leftover, meaningless. This is also wrong, because the arrangement is not random. It has a shape that fits its environment too well.

**The correct answer is that the policy has a free-floating rationale.** It persists because it has a consequence — concentrating a benefit on a small group that will fight for it, dispersing the cost across a large group that will not — and that consequence is what a selection process rewards. Policies with that shape survive the turnover of governments the way the cuckoo's shove survives the turnover of cuckoos. Nobody needs to intend the shape. The system finds it.

The cuckoo's reason is "no rivals for the food." The policy's reason is "a defender for every unit of benefit and no defender for the cost." Both are correct, both fully explain what we see, and both live in no one's head.

## What This Does to the Argument

If you accept this, a great deal of political conversation stops making sense.

"Who wanted this?" becomes the wrong question, not because it has no answer but because its answer does not explain anything. The right question is: *what property of this arrangement makes it survive in this system?* That is a question about the system, not about the people in it, and it has the same answer regardless of who is elected.

It also explains a frustration everyone has felt. You replace the officeholder, and the policy stays. You expose the lobbyist, and the policy stays. You elect a reformer who campaigned against it, and the reformer, in office, discovers reasons to keep it. None of this requires corruption. It requires only that the arrangement has a rationale that the system rewards, and that the individuals passing through are less durable than the arrangement.

## The Conclusion Dennett Would Not Draw

Dennett is a philosopher of biology and stops at the description. I want to go one step further.

If the policies that persist are the ones with free-floating rationales, and those rationales are about the shape of the selecting environment, then changing outcomes means **changing the selecting environment** — not the people in it. A system in which the cost-bearers could veto a policy as easily as the beneficiaries can defend it would select for different shapes. That is a claim about design, and it is why I keep returning to the question of how decisions are made rather than who makes them.

The cuckoo chick is not evil. It is a chick in a nest that rewards shoving. Move it to a nest that does not, and the shoving disappears — not because the chick was persuaded, but because the reason stopped floating.
